Building a prototype of a continence goal-selection tool for children with spina bifida: 
1 

patient, parental and urology provider recommendations 
2 

 
3 

Summary 
4 

Introduction: No tools exist to help children with spina bifida (SB) describe their incontinence 
5 

concerns and to help their providers account for them when evaluating management success. 
6 

Our aim was to understand patients’, parents’, and clinicians’ preferences about how a SB 
7 

continence goal-selection tool (MyGoal-C) should look and function, and to create a tool 
8 

prototype. 
9 

 
10 

Methods: We used a qualitative research approach integrated with human-centered design 
11 

methods. We recruited children with SB (8-17 years old), parents (>=18 years old) of children 
12 

with SB (8-17 years old) in clinic and online, and urology healthcare providers at our institution. 
13 

We surveyed children and parents, and conducted parent and provider Zoom-based prototyping 
14 

sessions to iteratively design the app. Design researchers analyzed online activities using 
15 

affinity diagramming, group analysis and modeling activities. Provider sessions were analyzed 
16 

with qualitative thematic analysis based on grounded theory. Recruitment continued until 
17 

saturation was reached. 
18 

 
19 

Results: Thirteen children with SB participated (median age: 10 years old, 4 female, 9 shunted, 
20 

10 using bladder catheterizations). Thirty-seven parents participated (33 mothers, median age: 
21 

43 years old). Children and parents unanimously recommended an app and their comments 
22 

generated 6 major theme domains: goal-setting process, in-app content, working toward goals, 
23 

urology provider check-in, app customization, and using big data to improve future functionality. 
24 

Twenty-one of the parents participated in 3 prototyping sessions with 6 breakout groups. The 
25 

remaining 16 parents and 13 children then completed the Prototype Testing Survey, leading to a 
26

refined prototype and a visual flow map of the app experience (Figure). Feedback from 11 
27 

urology healthcare providers (7 female, 6 advanced practice providers) generated 8 themes: 
28 

patient engagement/autonomy, clearly displaying results, integration into clinic workflow, not 
29 

increasing clinical staff burden, potential clinician bias, parental involvement, limitations of the 
30 

app, and future app adaptation. These cumulative data allowed for a construction of a final app 
31 

prototype. 
32 

 
33 

Comment: Findings of our study lay the foundation for creating a goal-selection app that meets 
34 

preferences and needs of children, parents, and providers. Next steps involve building the app, 
35 

testing its usability and assessing it prospectively in a clinical setting. 
36 

 
37 

Conclusions: Children with SB and parents preferred an app to help them set and track 
38 

continence goals. All stakeholders, including urology providers, offered complementary and 
39 

mutually reinforcing feedback to guide the creation of an app prototype that would ultimately be 
40 

integrated into a clinic visit.

Introduction  
49 

Most children with spina bifida (SB) experience urinary and fecal incontinence (UI and 
50 

FI)[1,2]. Both of these issues are typically managed by a urologist. In addition to maintaining 
51 

renal health, one of the urologist’s main goals during an office visit is to identify which patients 
52 

care about incontinence, to what degree they care, and what their expectations are. 
53 

Unfortunately, no tools exist to help patients and healthcare providers systematically delineate 
54 

patient’s concerns and to subsequently take them into account when evaluating management 
55 

success[3,4]. Given this knowledge gap, we set out to develop a patient-centered UI and FI 
56 

continence goal-selection MyGoal-C tool for children with SB to assist in shared decision 
57 

making (SDM) between patients, parents and clinicians[5].  
58 

Our recent work [5] corroborated evidence that children with SB consider UI and FI to be 
59 

closely linked and to be related to self-esteem, independence, relationships and social 
60 

activities[6,7]. By identifying specific continence goals affecting areas of life most important to a 
61 

child, the healthcare team can help the child achieve goals with the greatest potential impact on 
62 

their health-related quality of life (HRQOL). To maximize the future use of a tool by children, 
63 

parents and healthcare providers, it is critical to engage them throughout the research and 
64 

development process, making the intervention truly patient-centered[8]. Therefore, we 
65 

collaborated with a design research team to facilitate the research process with patients, 
66 

parents, researchers, and healthcare providers[9]. 
67 

The current study aimed to employ a qualitative research approach integrating design 
68 

methods to understand patients’, parents’, and clinicians’ preferences about how the goal-
69 

selection tool should look, how it should function and to create a prototype. Subsequent work 
70 

will involve a quantitative evaluation of the tool focused on usability and effectiveness, including 
71 

HRQOL. 
72 

 
73 

Methods  
74

5 
 

Approach 
75 

We partnered with Research Jam (RJ), the Indiana Clinical and Translation Science 
76 

Institutes’ Patient Engagement Core, which has expertise in applying human-centered design 
77 

research techniques to health services research. RJ has successfully created multiple patient-
78 

centered research interventions in the past[9–11]. Design methods augment traditional 
79 

qualitative tool development, improving the understanding of people’s needs by often going 
80 

beyond what people themselves expect[9,12]. It ensures that the end products are “co-
81 

designed” by all stakeholders. 
82 

 
83 

Participants  
84 

Children with SB (8-17 years old) living in the United States were recruited at our 
85 

center’s pediatric SB clinic and online through the Spina Bifida Indiana and national Spina Bifida 
86 

Association, and the “Bowel Management in Spina Bifida” Facebook group. Interested children 
87 

and parents recruited in clinic were contacted via telephone to discuss participation. Those 
88 

recruited online read the online study information sheet. Parent/guardian participants (>=18 
89 

years old) whose children had SB (>=8 years old) were recruited similarly. Pediatric urology 
90 

healthcare providers at our institution were invited by email to participate in a focus group. 
91 

Children’s assent and parental and provider consent were obtained online. Children and parents 
92 

received $50 for completing each study component, providers received $20 (Institutional Review 
93 

Board approval: 1707207063). 
94 

 
95 

Data collection 
96 

Data collection occurred in four iterative stages: an initial online Qualtrics-based toolkit 
97 

for children and parents, an online Zoom prototyping design sessions with parents, a Qualtrics-
98 

based Prototyping Testing Survey for children and parents (Online Supplement), and a focus 
99

group discussion with pediatric urology healthcare providers. Recruitment continued from July 
100 

2020 to March 2021 until thematic saturation was reached (no new themes were identified)[13]. 
101 

The initial online toolkit included activities to inform the goal-setting tool messaging. 
102 

Parents were invited to “Build a motivational message for your child” by selecting from a set of 
103 

images and writing captions. Children were similarly invited to “Build a message to give advice 
104 

to others your age with spina bifida.”  
105 

During Zoom design sessions, parents in facilitated breakout groups brainstormed ideas 
106 

for and then prototyped several goal-setting tools using an online whiteboarding tool (Miro, 
107 

miro.com). At the end of each session, the breakout groups came together to review and 
108 

discuss the prototypes of the other groups. 
109 

Based on analysis of the design sessions, the team created visualizations of a 
110 

preliminary prototype. In the Prototype Testing Survey, children and parents were shown a 
111 

visual flow map (Figure) and an overview of how the app would work. They were asked what 
112 

they liked, what they would change, and what might prevent them from using it. Participants 
113 

were also taken through a challenge mapping goals-setting prototype[14] to create incontinence 
114 

goals. This involved several prompts with free-text answers to populate a preformatted goal 
115 

phrase to yield a highly personalized goal. A refined prototype was created. 
116 

During provider Zoom sessions, urology providers were presented with a visual flow map 
117 

and refined prototype. They were invited to describe how to improve it to best fit into clinic 
118 

workflow, address provider preferences, and work for a variety of patient-provider encounter 
119 

scenarios.  
120 

 
121 

Outcomes 
122 

Our objectives were to determine the nature of the goals-selection tool, user 
123 

recommendations for the tool’s structure and function, and to develop a prototype to guide 
124 

future tool construction. 
125

7 
 

Analysis 
126 

Content from the prototypes (which were created in Miro during the design sessions) 
127 

was copied onto digital sticky notes. Deidentified content from the design session transcripts 
128 

was also copied to sticky notes. Data were analyzed in Miro by 4-5 team members over four 2-
129 

hour video meetings. The team used affinity diagramming, which involved iteratively grouping 
130 

notes based on similar content[15]. Headings describing their content were given to each group, 
131 

later becoming themes and theme domains. Headings were reviewed and revised through 
132 

group discussion. 
133 

The team brainstormed potential visual models that could be used to explore the data 
134 

and externalize internal mental models emerging through the research process. Each team 
135 

member received a set of sticky notes containing the headings derived from affinity 
136 

diagramming and created visual models. An experience map was selected to visually represent 
137 

a person’s journey using the tool and to find potential improvement opportunities[16]. 
138 

The pediatric urology provider discussion was audio recorded, transcribed, and 
139 

iteratively analyzed based on a systematic grounded theory approach[17,18]. Since the research 
140 

question focuses on describing a process involving individuals app use steps, a grounded 
141 

theory approach was chosen for this study. Transcripts were used to extract and rephrase 
142 

significant phrases in more general language and iteratively derive meanings to identifying 
143 

subsequent independent and exhaustive themes by two coders. Separate audio recording 
144 

transcript and interview codes documents were used along an alphanumeric category coding 
145 

system. Discrepancies were settled by consulting the audio recordings and consensus.  
146

Results 
148 

Child and parent population characteristics 
149 

Thirteen children participated (nine males) at a median age of 10 years old (Table 1). 
150 

Parents of all children participated in the parent portion of the study. Eleven were white, nine 
151

had shunted hydrocephalus, and six were community ambulators. Ten managed their bladder 
152 

with clean intermittent catheterizations and four used a MACE/Chait tube. Thirty-seven parents 
153 

participated (33 mothers) at a median age of 43 years old (Table 1). No couples participated. 
154 

Thirteen parents were white. Seventeen of their children with SB were female with a median 
155 

age of 15 years old. Twenty children had shunted hydrocephalus, 22 were community 
156 

ambulators, 25 managed their bladder with catheterizations, and 14 used a MACE/Chait tube.  
157 

 
158 

What should the goal-setting tool look? 
159 

 
Children and parents unanimously recommended a goals-setting smartphone app. They 
160 

raised concerns that a webpage or a paper-based aid would lack the portability to accompany 
161 

the patient in different settings (home, travel, trips, school, etc.), the versatility of always being at 
162 

hand (for reminders, recording progress, etc.), and the adaptability to individual preferences (for 
163 

customized goals, reminders, etc.).  
164 

 
165 

How should the app work? 
166 

Twenty-one parents participated in three prototyping sessions (2 hours each) with six 
167 

breakout groups. Another 16 parents and 13 children completed the Prototype Testing Survey. 
168 

Several themes emerged from comments provided by children and parents (Table 2), leading to 
169 

a visual flow map of the app experience (Figure). Emerging themes grouped into six domains 
170 

briefly described here. (1) Goal-setting process themes included: providing goal ideas to select 
171 

along with patient-generated goals, giving parents and healthcare providers input in goal-
172 

selection, goals should address the most immediate needs first, the app should generate a 
173 

practical plan to achieve goals, and teaching patients goal-setting skills over time. (2) In-app 
174 

content themes included: education, social support/tips, and resources specific to goals. (3) 
175 

Working toward goals themes included: tracking, reminders, and rewards. Remaining domains 
176

9 
 

were (4) Urology provider check-in, (5) App customization, and (6) Using big data features to 
177 

improve future functionality and recommendations. 
178 

 
179 

Parents imagining the goal-setting process 
180 

During all three co-design sessions, parents focused on implementing goals more than 
181 

setting them. When asked pointedly about how a patient would determine a goal, parents 
182 

suggested discussions with their doctor. Since this is not feasible in an app, we prototyped an 
183 

automated goal-selection approach in the Prototype Testing Survey using modified challenge 
184 

mapping. An example of a generated goal, with phrases filled out by participant in italics, was: 
185 

“You will pay close attention when I need to be cathed or have reminders, telling myself it’s not 
186 

the end of the world to finish the thing. This will help you have less accidents so that you cannot 
187 

waste time cleaning up accidents and changing clothes.” The final goals created using this 
188 

approach were impractical due to confusing grammar, unclear or overly complex answers, 
189 

unsolvable problems (question: “how can I/my child control his/her bladder?” answer: “not 
190 

possible”), trouble diverging (limited final options), and goals which were not actionable (not 
191 

measurable or time-based). 
192 

Building on our earlier work[5], we selected a modifiable menu-based approach for goal-
193 

setting. Children and parents endorsed this approach in the Prototype Testing Survey as 
194 

appropriate, easy to follow, and in agreement with their recommendations to include 
195 

prepopulated, but editable, goals in the app (Table 2).  
196 

 
197 

Pediatric urology provider themes 
198 

Eleven healthcare professionals participated (7 females) (Table 1). All were white. Five 
199 

were urologists, six were advanced practice providers. Four had more than 10 years of 
200 

experience caring for children with SB (four had 5-10 years, three had <5 years). Discussion 
201 

during two sessions (1.5 hours each) generated several themes: 
202

(1) Patient engagement and autonomy. The app may “help train patients in taking up 
203 

more responsibility for their care” by collaboratively setting expectations and tracking goals. The 
204 

app can facilitate communication between patients, families, and providers, allowing for more 
205 

personalized therapies to be selected and tracked. Users are already familiar with a smartphone 
206 

platform, making adoption easier. An in-app system of points or awards for accomplishing goals, 
207 

possibly with competition between users, may optimize patient motivation. Educational content 
208 

(troubleshooting common continence problems, testimonials, etc.) may be help engage children 
209 

and families. Collaborating with external groups or associations may facilitate this. While 
210 

important, educational content should be a secondary priority while the app undergoes initial 
211 

tested. 
212 

(2) Clearly displaying results. Progress made in targeted behaviors, goal achievement 
213 

and quality of life should be displayed in an easy to interpret fashion (single screen or printout) 
214 

to facilitate discussion with the family. It could then be scanned into the medical record.  
215 

(3) Integration into clinic workflow. The app should inform, rather than interfere with, 
216 

work in an already busy clinic. Goal-selection should be initiated by the child and parent(s) prior 
217 

to the clinic visit and finalized with the provider.  
218 

(4) App should not increase work burden on clinical staff between clinic visits. The app 
219 

should ideally improve communication with families during appointments without increasing 
220 

phone calls to the office. App troubleshooting should be outsourced. Given existing modes of 
221 

communication, clinicians did not support direct in-app messaging between patients and clinic 
222 

staff.  
223 

(5) Minimizing clinician bias. Periodically collecting data for subsequent review by 
224 

healthcare providers may bias users to provide responses that “please the provider.” But the 
225 

risk of this should be low, since the data is collected in the setting of their daily life, responses 
226 

are visible to patients for real-time feedback and this approach is an extrapolation of data 
227 

already collected at regular clinic visits.  
228

11 
 

(6) Acknowledging parental involvement. Since parents will be involved in using the app, 
229 

it will be difficult to separate the child’s input from their parents’. While emphasis will be on the 
230 

child providing most of the information in the app, parental involvement mirrors the reality that 
231 

parents are significantly engaged in the urological management of children with SB. 
232 

(7) Limitations of smartphone app. Children without a smartphone will be unable to use 
233 

the app, potentially limiting its use during school hours or by low income individuals.  
234 

(8) Adaptation and expansion of the app. The app should be periodically updated using 
235 

feedback from users. A similar app would be useful in the care of adults with SB who struggle 
236 

with similar continence concerns in a more complex personal and social context. If successful, 
237 

the app could be useful for non-urological SB care and other disease processes.

App prototype 
240 

 
Incorporating feedback from all stakeholders, a final prototype of the app was developed 
241 

(Figure 1). We also developed a mockup output of tracked results which would be available for 
242 

the patient, parent and the clinician to discuss during the clinic visit and incorporate in the 
243 

medical record (Figure 2).

245 

Discussion 
246 

Based on the recommendations of children with SB, parents, and urology healthcare 
247 

providers, this study generated a prototype of a patient-centered continence goals-selection 
248 

app. While the domains and themes we identified reflected each stakeholder group’s 
249 

viewpoints, virtually all of them were complementary, aiming at an app with maximal benefit and 
250 

minimal burden to users. Some recommendations appeared conflicted, such as those regarding 
251 

integrating the app into a clinical care. Patients and parents suggested potential additional goal-
252 

setting visits and an in-app provider chat function. Providers preferred integrating the app into 
253 

existing visits and relying on existing patient/parent-provider communication. Efficiently 
254

integrating the app into the current workflow will be critical to successful implementation, 
255 

especially outside the research setting. 
256 

Smartphone apps present an attractive low-cost intervention to reach a wide population 
257 

to augment typical care. While the number of pediatric healthcare apps is growing, the use of 
258 

this interactive mobile technology in continence goal setting is new. Many, especially early, apps 
259 

pertaining to physical and mental health tend to focus on symptom reporting and did not to 
260 

include goal setting or treatment content[19,20]. The next generation of clinical pediatric apps 
261 

are undergoing randomized controlled testing for problems ranging from childhood obesity 
262 

[21]and oral health[22], to cognitive behavioral therapy [23] and pain management in sickle cell 
263 

disease[24]. Similarly, we hope the MyGoal-C app will help improve care of children with SB. To 
264 

optimize future user experience and “gamification,” we anticipate further updating the MyGoal-C 
265 

app based on patterns of use.  This includes updating goal selection approaches based on 
266 

goals actually selected and may involve adding the possibility of competing with other users, or 
267 

themselves, with accumulated awards for achieved goals. 
268 

The next steps in the study involve the construction of the app, testing in the clinic and 
269 

evaluating its user-friendliness, or usability [25]. While usability testing typically occurs in areas 
270 

of engineering and computer-human interaction, it is only emerging in healthcare. Afterwards, 
271 

we will conduct a “before-after” prospective study of a cohort of children with SB to quantitatively 
272 

assess patient-reported outcomes before and after implementing the MyGoal-C app to 
273 

determine how treated UI impacts HRQOL. Recruitment for this study’s “before” phase has 
274 

begun. Once operationalized and prospectively validated, our hope is to make the app available 
275 

to patients outside our center. 
276 

The study has several limitations. While we initially planned to rely mostly on children’s 
277 

input, it was not surprising that children were uninterested in sharing intimate details of UI and 
278 

FI with researchers they did not know. We pivoted to engage children with online surveys and 
279 

engaged parents and healthcare providers in group discussions. Despite a heterogenous study 
280

13 
 

population representing varying levels of disability and locations of urological care, parents 
281 

participating were English-speaking, predominantly white mothers. We did not capture the 
282 

educational level or health literacy of study participants. Despite its small size, our sample is 
283 

typical of qualitative studies, and we will recruit new groups of participants in subsequent study 
284 

stages. The readability level of the functional app could not be assessed since it is currently 
285 

being constructed. Subsequent work will include an evaluation of any differences in app use and 
286 

effectiveness based on age and sex. In the future, we hope to develop a related app for adults 
287 

with SB. Importantly, we plan to periodically update and customize the app based on users’ 
288 

input data and feedback to continue crafting a patient-centered tool to help in clinical decision 
289 

making. 
290 

 
291 

Conclusions 
292 

 
Children with SB and parents preferred an app to help them set and track continence 
293 

goals. All stakeholders, including urology providers, offered complementary and mutually 
294 

reinforcing feedback to guide the creation of an app prototype integrated into the workflow of a 
295 

typical clinic visit.
