2. Methods 
This article describes the design, development, and evaluation of the eACT mHealth 
learning environment. eACT was developed by an interdisciplinary team of experts in 3 phases: 
1) preparation, 2) iterative design, and 3) iterative development. Preparation included developing 
user personas and conducting focus groups. Iterative design made use of rapid prototypes to 
revise the product between versions. Iterative development continued refinement to create and 
test alpha, beta, and gold releases. Research was approved by the institutional review boards at 
partnering institutions (Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center, Medical University of 
South Carolina, and Children’s Hospital of Orange County). The research focused on the 
following questions: 
 
(RQ1) How can the user-centered methods of empathy interviews, empathy 
mapping, and persona development inform design principles for an mHealth online 
learning environment for parents/caregivers of CWE? 
 
(RQ2) How can identified design principles be incorporated into an operable 
design framework for an mHealth online learning environment for parents/caregivers of 
CWE? 
 
(RQ3) How do participants perceive the usability and user experience of the 
mHealth online learning environment, and what might be improved?

2.1. Successive Approximation Model 
Design was guided using Allen’s [14] Successive Approximation Model version 2.0 
(SAM2; Figure 2) across 3 phases. Phase 1 used empathy interviews, empathy mapping, and 
persona development methods. Empathy interviews allow designers to develop empathy with 
target users [15]. Empathy mapping is used to distill themes from empathy interviews. This 
results in information that can be used to create “personas”, fictional archetypes of users [16]. 
Phase 2 used rapid prototyping to incorporate user, pedagogical, and technological 
considerations from Phase 1, leading to a final design proof that combined content and 
multimedia prototypes with a system architecture. In Phase 3, three product releases were 
developed and tested (alpha, beta, and gold). Usability and user experience methods were used, 
including SME reviews, cognitive walkthroughs, and usability testing.

2.2.1 Sampling 
A purposeful sampling strategy was used in Phase 1 so as to recruit a diverse group of 
parents of CWE across a range of ages and races whose children had a variety of epilepsy types. 
A total of 9 participants were recruited (black/African American=2; more than 1 race=2; 
white=5), the majority of whom were female (n=8). Epilepsy types included localization related 
epilepsy (n=1), frontal lobe epilepsy (n=1), childhood absence epilepsy (n=1) and unclassified 
epilepsy (n=6). All 9 participants were scheduled for interviews; however, only 4 attended the 
scheduled sessions, as detailed in section 3.1.1 below.  
Purposeful sampling was also employed in Phase 3 seeking parents of CWE in the age 
range of 2-12. This age range was selected, as we sought to focus on caregivers who are 
primarily responsible for medication management. Prior studies from which this study was 
derived focused on 2-12 year olds, so the same age range was kept. Around 12 years old, 
adolescents begin managing their own medication regimens; hence, older children were excluded 
so as to provide a family-focused intervention with the ability to influence adherence in younger 
age groups. 
 
2.3. Analysis

2.3.1. Analysis of Empathy Interviews (Phase 1) 
Empathy maps were created using information from empathy interviews (Figure 3), 
focusing on four points of distinction (say, think, do, feel). Four empathy maps were completed 
and then translated into 8 representative user personas.

2.3.2. Analysis of Usage Test Data (Phase 3) 
Ease-of-use, relevance of content, appeal of visual design, and efficiency of navigation 
were evaluated. Small sample sizes are sufficient to identify the majority of usability issues 
[19,20]. Sampling procedures were based on this standard. Usability testing was conducted with 
8 purposefully sampled caregivers, none of whom were involved in empathy interviews in Phase 
1. Issues that negatively impacted user experience were rectified between sessions. Each 
participant evaluated at least two modules. 
 
2.3.2.1. Quantitative Analysis  
System Usability Scale (SUS) and Adjectival Rating Scale data were analyzed using 
quantitative methods. SUS results were calculated using methods outlined by Brooke [17]. 
Scores of 68 are considered to represent above average usability. Data were aggregated into 
spreadsheet tables for analysis. 
 
2.3.2.2. Qualitative Analysis  
Qualitative analysis focused on identifying features of the intervention that promoted or 
hindered the system’s ease-of-use and participants’ user experiences. Analysis was based on 
Kushniruk & Borycki’s [21] existing coding framework designed for medical intervention 
contexts and extended based on usability standards [22]. Coding agreement was calculated using

3. Results & Discussion 
This study formatively evaluated eACT, considering how user-centered methods might 
inform design principles, how design principles might be incorporated into an operable design 
framework, how participants perceived usability and user experience, and what might be 
improved. Considerations of efficacy were outside the scope of the current study. 
 
3.1. Results from Phase 1: Preparation (RQ1)

3.1.1. Phase 1 Participant Demographics 
Participants were parents of children between 2 and 12 years of age with a range of 
epilepsy diagnoses. Recruitment was performed at a large Midwestern children’s hospital. 
Participants were provided with clear study procedures, benefits, and potential risks. Four 
caregivers participated in Phase 1 data collection (Table 5).

maps (Figure 4). Insights were segmented using the following criteria: (a) say, (b) think, (c) do,

3.2. Results of Phase 2: Iterative Design (RQ2) 
In Phase 2, various needs-based decisions were made, such as selecting a delivery 
system, evaluative metrics, and instructional strategies appropriate to the learner [23]. This 
required that we marry learning context with user-experience design principles to promote 
transfer of learning to daily living contexts [24]. Three focus areas for subsequent design 
emerged: 1) user considerations, 2) pedagogical considerations, and 3) technological 
considerations (Table 6).  
 
***Insert Table 6 about here*** 
 
3.2.1. User Considerations 
Three design principles were articulated related to user considerations:  
1. Caregivers’ Needs: Design to meet the needs and sentiments of parents/caregivers of 
CWE;  
2. Adult Learning: Deliver content in a brief, readily-accessible format that can be 
completed on the go to meet demands of busy adult learners; and  
3. Content: Promote relevancy and trustworthiness in content and messaging.

Outcomes suggested that caregivers of CWE would like to feel a sense of normalcy; 
therefore, we incorporated content and infographics that addressed, for example, common myths 
about epilepsy. This was presented efficiently in <5 minute microlearning lessons. Content 
promoting positive language and thinking, trustworthiness, authority, accuracy, and relevancy 
was also incorporated. 
 
3.2.2 Pedagogical Considerations 
User feedback suggested that content should be trustworthy and come from an authority; 
therefore, the ASK system [25] included the avatar of a provider to represent an expert. 
Participants were not familiar with the provider (“Dr. Modi”) whose avatar and name were used 
in the app. Design and engagement strategies were also implemented that accounted for adult 
learning theories and busy lifestyles [26,27]. Microlearning units [28,29] were designed to be 
highly interactive, yet short. Progress tracking allowed caregivers to stop and start at will and 
displayed progress.  
 
3.2.3. Technological Considerations 
eACT was housed on a HIPAA-compliant server. W3C and web accessibility standards 
were incorporated throughout the system [30]. Features included private user accounts, progress 
tracking, and a litany of multimedia content. eACT uses responsive design and can be accessed 
on devices other than smartphones, such as laptops and tablets.  
 
3.3. Results of Phase 3: Iterative Development (RQ3) 
Phase 3 addressed how participants perceived the usability and user experience of eACT 
and potential areas of improvement. Analysis consisted of multimodal user testing and ongoing 
refinements following each round of evaluation [19,31]. Survey responses and usability feedback 
were used to further refine the intervention [32] across multiple releases [14].

Participants rated usability of eACT highly (Table 8). The lowest calculated score (65)

3.3.3. Qualitative Findings 
Qualitative results fell into two major categories: (1) usability issues and (2) features that 
promoted usability (Table 9). Usability issues included challenges with navigation, confusion 
about system features, and inconsistencies in our interface design. Features that promoted 
usability included short modules, straight-to-the-point answers to ASK questions, progress 
monitoring, the avatar presented as an authoritative medical provider, and use of engaging 
multimedia. Analysis of usage test videos found that participants’ experiences were largely 
positive and free of usability challenges. Some infographic and visual details were found to be 
unclear. User experience was free of major bugs or glitches. Identified usability issues were 
limited and of low severity. All identified usability flaws were remediated between testing 
sessions.

3.3.3.1. Parents/Caregivers Needs 
Qualitative findings suggest participants recognized eACT was designed to take their 
needs as being a parent or caregiver of a CWE into consideration. “Renee” said, “it's nice to 
know that you're not the only one in the world struggling to give your kid their medicine.” 
Another parent described talking with a nurse practitioner about the, “difficulties in adherence, 
the medication schedule.” Yet another talked about her daughter’s forgetfulness in taking her 
medication and described how the ‘stigma’ associated with medical adherence was challenging 
and that resources like this helped her realize that, “this is not something to be ashamed of, this is 
not your fault...is good for parents to keep seeing that and for them to keep telling their kids 
that.” Participants also stated that they wished they had resources like eACT when they first 
learned of their child’s diagnosis. “Alyson” said, “I think the information is helpful...when we 
first started out, this type of information would have been very helpful." 
 
3.3.3.2. Adult Learning 
Findings indicate the design of the system aligned with the needs of adult learners. For 
instance, “Renee” told us that the use of short lessons and units "helps parents not feel 
like...overwhelmed” and that it was helpful that they will “not have to sit down for hours at a 
time, and try to figure this out.” They said this helped to make the use of the eACT mHealth 
learning environment “feel more obtainable.” Others pointed out how easy the interface was to 
use. Specifically, “Cathy” said that the content was, "stated appropriately; it was stated so 
anyone could understand it… Clear and concise, giving them just what they ask for, and just 
what they needed." The use of infographics and visual information was found to promote a 
positive learning experience for participants, with multiple participants commenting on the 
inclusion of infographics, such as “Fred” saying, "This graphic right here, I really like

because...this graphic gives a lot of detail in a very structured, almost chronological way." “Sam” 
said, “I like how it's [the infographics] presented. I like that it's short too, but to the point.” 
 
3.3.3.3. Content 
Findings suggest participants found content to be trustworthy, relevant, digestible, and 
positive. “Moriah” described how the use of infographics made content easier to explain to their 
child. She described how it was hard to communicate what a seizure is and why taking medicine 
was important. During the usage test “Moriah” said, "I like this [infographic], it's hard to explain 
to a child, or anyone who doesn't know about epilepsy or what a seizure is. This gives, ah, 
something that is more relatable, an answer to somebody...that's something good that I can tell 
my child who is balking about why she has to take medicine...I like this analogy." Content was 
also found to be reliable and trustworthy. “Fred” told us how they had done research for years 
and often got “confused” because they were “not a doctor.” “Moriah” said the use of our doctor 
avatars were able to, “give you some comfort [avatar]...that this [epilepsy] is treatable by a 
medical profession. So I feel more comfortable seeing a picture of a physician, rather than just a 
random picture." Related to relevance to parents, “Cathy” said the content was, "very concise, so 
I didn't have to read five million pages just to get that little bit of information I needed." 
 
3.3.3.4. Summary of Qualitative Findings 
Taken together, findings suggest that eACT helped 1) promote a sense of normalcy about 
their situation, 2) provide strategies for coordinating medication adherence across multiple 
caregivers, and 3) provide ways to reduce stressful and negative feelings that rise up due to their 
circumstances. eACT embodied adult learning strategies, including mobile microlearning 
principles, a simple and easy-to-use interface, the ability for users to stop and restart where they 
left off, and the inclusion of infographics. Finally, the system was found to be trustworthy, 
relevant, straightforward, and positive.  
 
3.4. Limitations 
The findings presented here should be interpreted in light of the limitations detailed in 
this section. Due to the nature of user-centered design, findings cannot be generalized beyond the 
context of the current study. Instead, our study uses small sample sizes and focuses on user 
experience from a single context and does not seek to create generalizable knowledge. Further 
limitations may result from our subject pool given that research participants were nearly all 
white, female-identified, and the majority of whom had children with generalized seizures. 
Consideration of how these characteristics might have impacted the findings was outside the 
scope of the current study, suggesting a need for further research. The purpose was to create and 
refine an innovative, acceptable, and practical intervention that was specifically tailored for the 
target population. Thus, this paper reveals new insights about design decisions that seek to 
address the needs of the target population. However, questions remain regarding the extent to 
which children’s type of epilepsy and/or the frequency/severity of seizures might influence 
particular parent/caregiver needs and sentiments.

4. Conclusion 
User-centered design methods enabled creation of an mHealth intervention that was 
highly relevant and usable to end-users. Traditionally, development of instructional systems 
tends to focus on technological usability [19]. However, by engaging in this learner-centric 
process, we instead were able to more carefully examine social, technological, and pedagogical 
dimensions of usability. Future researchers and learning designers will benefit from similarly 
considering how technology impacts pedagogical and social dimensions of learning [33]. 
Furthermore, research suggests that certain aspects of pedagogical agent representation, for 
example, avatar realism, avatar animations, and non-traditional representation of avatars (i.e., 
African-American, female), can influence acquisition and transfer of learning, learner self-
regulation, and learner self-efficacy [35]. Therefore, researchers might consider in future studies 
the extent to which the specific representation of pedagogical agent avatars might influence 
perceived trustworthiness, authority, and competence (e.g., female-presenting versus not, 
physician of color versus not).  
 At a glance, it may appear that many of the findings and design insights provided herein 
might apply equally well to other chronic or stigmatizing health conditions. What makes these 
findings unique to epilepsy as a condition and parents/caregivers as a group has to do with the 
unique nature of epilepsy. For example, the microlearning content is focused only on epilepsy 
and is specifically attuned to the somewhat inexact nature of the science of epilepsy, which 
sometimes requires trial-and-error around drugs and dosing that does not exist for other 
populations and conditions. Epilepsy is also a condition in which participants can be weaned 
from anti-epileptic medications if they are seizure free for two years. Anti-epileptic medication 
adherence is the greatest predictor of seizure freedom. Therefore, unlike other chronic conditions 
like type 1 diabetes or sickle cell disease, adherence behaviors such as those promoted by the 
eACT intervention have great potential impact long-term outcomes and need for future 
treatment.  
As designers consider how user experience design practices can be included in the design 
of healthcare-oriented learning systems, it is important that practitioners gain a better 
understanding of target users and how designs can be sensitive to user-specific socio-contextual 
factors [34]. As part of this process, there is a need for design cases that bring forth and share 
how user-centered design strategies are being utilized to gather the needs and insights of end-
users to inform the design process [24]. This paper provides such insight into how formative user 
experience design processes can be deployed for developing highly relevant and usable learning 
interventions for adult learners. Ongoing randomized controlled trial research is currently 
underway to examine the efficacy of eACT.
