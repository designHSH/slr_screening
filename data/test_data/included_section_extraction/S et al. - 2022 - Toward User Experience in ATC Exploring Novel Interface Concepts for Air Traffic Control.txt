Toward User Experience in ATC:
Exploring Novel Interface Concepts
for Air Traffic Control

Stephan Huber1,*, Johanna Gramlich1, Selina Pauli1,
Simon Mundschenk1, Eliana Haugg2 and Tobias Grundgeiger1

1Psychological Ergonomics, University of Würzburg, 97070 Würzburg, Germany
2Research & Development, Deutsche Flugsicherung GmbH, 63225 Langen, Germany

As part of a larger project, we followed a contextual design
(Holtzblatt and Beyer, 2016) process in ATC. In this work,
we focused on designing and evaluating new interaction tech-
niques for future ATC systems. We strived to develop a design,
based on controllers’ psychological needs and experiences in
the context of their work. We developed a unified view on
air traffic with three different prototypes, a mouse interface,
a flight-dial and tangible models. We formatively evaluated
those prototypes with air traffic controllers to explore, how
different interaction techniques can support their work. Dur-
ing this process, we aimed to understand ATC as a holistic
experience and to design for positive UX beyond mere task
fulfillment and efficiency. We rounded off the design process
with a summative evaluation, to test the UX of our prototypes
against the system currently in use. The aim of the study was
to identify which aspects of UX improved as a result of the
novel interaction concepts. Quantifying aspects of UX beyond
usability (Gürlük et al., 2014) is a novel contribution in the
context of ATC. Finally, we discussed our quantitative findings,
as well as insights and implications for the future workplace
in ATC.

2.
DESIGN PROCESS

Huber et al.’s (2020) work indicates that pilots insist on audio-
only communication during the approach. Hence, we limit
the workstation redesign and investigation of emerging UX
to approach control, leaving the pilots’ interface untouched.
Using the models and diagrams created by Huber et al. (2020),
we conducted two wall walk workshops (Holtzblatt and Beyer,
2016) with a total of 15 team members to generate key insights
and design ideas. All team members had backgrounds in
human-computer-interaction, psychology, design or informa-
tion technology, and all but two were involved in data collection
or had worked in the field of air traffic management.

2.1.
User needs

The qualitative data obtained during the contextual inquiries
included insights regarding organization, strategies, inter-
action, communication and life after work. Below, we list
the selection of top user needs for approach controllers—
the essence of our ethnographical work—that we considered
during prototyping and will refer to in the following sections.
Of course, these user needs are in addition to the workplace
priorities safety, structure, and efficiency:

1. Controllers value the direct, objective feedback repre-
sented by separation between aircraft on the final.
2. Controllers approach similar situations differently, that
is, their individual style varies.
3. Controllers want to be able to customize their worksta-
tion and interface.
4. Controllers want input devices to be compatible with one
other.
5. Controllers need information grouped in context when it
is required at the same time.
6. Controllers want a system that can be handled directly
and intuitively without workarounds.
7. Controllers want to set markers and vectors as visual aids.
8. Controllers need support but no replacement for their
skill and experience.
9. Controllers need strategies to stay focused at quiet night
shifts.

2.2.
Ideation and visioning

After the wall walks, we conducted three visioning workshops
as described by Holtzblatt and Beyer (2016) and iteratively
created different visions. To ensure a shared perspective on
user data during these workshops, every team member must
have participated in the wall walk. Visions may build on each
other and cover several user needs. While visioning is a creative
process that may include aspects that go beyond user data, we
evaluated all visions based on their conformity with user needs.
Building a final interface covering all needs is very visionary.
Instead, we aimed at rethinking the entire workstation, rather

than one screen that needs to fit in the existing workstation. The
objective was to envision an interface that addresses several
user needs without opposing others. In the present paper, we
focus on the vision one-face-to-the-ATC, because it reoccurred
in all workshops and was central in four visions.

One-face-to-the-ATC describes one unified interface that
allows the controller to work on all of their tasks in one
place. The one-face-to-the-ATC vision served as the general
framework for the specific interaction concepts because the
distinction between the radar display and the digital flight
strip display, and the general interaction concept of the current
workstation are no longer part of the envisioned workstation.
Within one-face-to-the-ATC vision, we came up with three
different interaction interfaces, which we describe in detail
in the next section: the mouse interface, the flight-dial and
tangible models.

3.
INTERFACE AND INTERACTION CONCEPTS

ATC is a visual domain with the ‘radar’ display currently
used to supervise movements of aircraft at its center (Fig. 1b).
To increase acceptance of our prototypes, we kept the visual
representation of air traffic as the core element and prototyped
three different concepts to interact with the traffic. The graphic
user interfaces of all three prototypes follow the vision one-
face-to-the-ATC. In contrast to the strip system, controllers
can perform all interactions directly on the radar screen of the
prototypes (Fig. 2, UN4–6). This reduces the need to divide
attention between monitors on desk level, on eye level and
above (Fig. 1), and allows controllers to focus their attention
on the radar picture at all times (UN1) while reducing so-called
head down time (i.e. the time in which the controller is not
looking at the radar screen to observe the air traffic).

Controllers can document new clearances directly in the
label. In detail, the label displays two rows with aircraft related
data (Fig. 3) and one row with context sensitive suggestions for
upcoming clearances (UN8). On the right side of the screen, we
left space for controllers to configure their preferred constella-
tion of information (UN3). Based on data from the contextual
inquiry, we assumed that controllers still required weather
information and an image of the situation on the ground to
pursue their tasks efficiently. To limit the permanently present
information inside the label, we added a representation of a
traditional flight strip in the bottom right corner of the screen
(Fig. 2d). In the following sections, we will explain the charac-
teristic interactions for each of the three interaction concepts.

3.1.
Mouse interface

The mouse interface is designed to be intuitive to use (UN6) for
people with prior experience of desktop computer setups. All
actions can be performed one handed with the mouse, allowing
the controllers to permanently hold the push-to-talk button of
the radio unit in the other hand (UN4). The mouse enables

controllers to manipulate clearances directly in the labels of the
aircraft (UN5, Fig. 3). When hovering over values in the third
row with the cursor, controllers can increment or decrement
suggested values via scrolling and can confirm a selection
by clicking (UN8). Confirmed values are underlined to show
they have been cleared. After giving an aircraft clearance for
the localizer or ILS, controllers can directly document this by
toggling the state of icons in the label. Distance vectors between
aircraft (UN7) are displayed by click-and-hold while drawing
a box around the aircraft of interest. We implemented the
mouse interface with the rapid prototyping software Axure RP9
(http://www.axure.com). The setup during user tests consisted
of a 24′′ screen and a wireless three-key mouse. The prototype
ran in a full screen browser (https://www.mozilla.org/) with
disabled smooth scrolling.

3.2.
Flight dial

The idea of using a dial emerged from the controllers’ needs
of a system that is intuitive to use, without any workarounds
(UN6) and offers consistent input methods (UN4). Navigating
through menus by rotating and clicking a dial is not a novel
approach. For instance, car manufacturers (Spreng, 2002) use
a dial as an input device to navigate their infotainment system.
Potential advantages of the flight-dial include being able to
quickly navigate menus through haptic feedback and muscle
memory (MacLean, 2000) and a minimized occlusion problem
(Bachl et al., 2010) opposed to the mouse cursor. The dial
allows the precise selection of neighboring values but causes
increased physical effort when scrolling over large distances.
Therefore, we tried to avoid rotations over 90 degrees in either
direction by suggesting realistic values for upcoming clear-
ances. Furthermore, the flight-dial interface is designed to be

used with both hands. While one hand always stays on the dial
(Fig. 2), the other hand (which also holds the radio push-to-
talk- button) selects aircraft via touch. We deliberately decided
to use the touchscreen solely for selecting aircraft and pressing
on-screen buttons and not for entering and confirming single
values via touch. The touch interaction should not be utilized
for extremely fine or accurate inputs in order to decrease
erroneous data entry in this safety-critical domain (Fitts, 1954).

Controllers can select an aircraft directly by touching its rep-
resentation on the screen. Alternatively, they can use the dial to
scroll through all aircraft assigned to them using a cursor in the
form of a blue border around the aircraft’s head symbol. Navi-
gating within an aircraft label is solely conducted using the dial.
Controllers can scroll through the suggested clearances and
click to select a specific type of clearance (e.g. altitude). Con-
trollers can then optionally manipulate the suggested values by
rotating the dial in either direction and clicking the dial once
more to confirm the adjusted values. Further features, like the
aircraft to aircraft vectors (i.e. a line connecting aircraft indicat-
ing the distance between them), are available in a semicircular
context menu that can be accessed by selecting an aircraft
and turning the dial counterclockwise (Fig. 4). The setup con-
sisted of Microsoft’s Surface Dial (https://microsoft.com/en-
us/p/surface-dial/925r551sktgn) and a 27′′ display with touch
functionality. From analog to the mouse interface, we built
the interface using the rapid prototyping software Axure RP9.
For a video of the interaction with the flight dial, see the
supplementary online material, FlightDial.mp4.

3.3.
Tangible models

Because paper strips have been replaced by the paperless strip
system, tangible elements are missing in ATC workspaces
(Huber et al., 2020). According to Hornecker (2005) tangible
interaction allows for bodily interaction with physical objects

embedded in a spatial context while connecting the physi-
cal and the virtual world. Our user research indicated the
controllers’ need for direct input options and simple interac-
tions and feedback. Therefore, we developed various tangible
models, which are abstract representations of aircraft and allow
for direct (UN1) interaction that has the potential to be intuitive
(UN6) through their adapted familiarity of an actual aircraft
that can be physically moved (Israel et al., 2009). Selecting
a tangible model that best suits the controllers’ ergonomic
needs additionally offers adjustability of the workspace (UN3).
The tangible models represent aircraft in a playful way, which
encourage the controllers to engage with the system in shifts
with low traffic (UN9). Based on insights from contextual
inquiries, we started building tangible models with modeling
material on a low fidelity level, to generate multiple ideas
for different tasks that can easily be modified or combined.
From the variety of models, we selected four shapes (Fig. 5)
that offered ergonomic forms to support multiple interaction
opportunities (UN2 and UN3), such as turning, tilting and
moving, which are based on current behavior or language

in the context of ATC and therefore familiar to controllers
(UN6). To increase fidelity, we iterated the designs using a 3D
printer with a flexible filament (PolyFlex TPU95, 1.75 mm),
which made the models slightly elastic. The models’ elasticity
offered passive haptic feedback and facilitated squeezing as an
additional form of interaction.

We assigned specific interactions with the tangible models
to four basic tasks, which controllers frequently execute: (a)
identifying an aircraft and changing its (b) flight level/altitude,
(c) speed, or (d) heading (see Fig. 6). We designed the tangible
props to encourage controllers to discuss about (i) how they
would use them to interact with flight data; (ii) share their
thoughts about size, material, and shape; and (iii) consider
the advantages and disadvantages of tangible models as a new
interaction technique in ATC. As additional stimulus material,
we produced a short video containing exemplary linkages
between interactions and tasks, together with visual anima-
tions (see supplementary online material, Tangibles.mp4). The
design process of the tangible models is reported in more detail
in (Gramlich et al., 2022).

4.
FORMATIVE EVALUATION

For a formative evaluation of the prototypes, we acquired 24
approach controllers from a control center of a larger European
air traffic service provider. All participants provided informed
and written consent. Participation in our evaluation took place
during regular work time, and no additional incentives were
offered. Sessions lasted approximately 1–1.5 hours, and each
participant had the opportunity to test all three prototypes.

The mouse interface required only a short explanation before
testing. Because the dial is a novel interaction concept, we
showed a short video that explained the prototype’s interac-
tions to participants, prior to a free exploration phase (see
supplementary online material, FlightDial.mp4). To test the
prototypes, we asked the participants to explore the interface
independently while simultaneously thinking aloud as to their
opinion of the prototype and its interaction with the flight data.
Subsequently, we asked more specific questions in relation to
individual features.

The tests with the tangibles were more explorative than those
with the mouse and flight dial. First, we asked the controllers to
choose a favorite model and show us their ideas for interaction,
then we showed the controllers the aforementioned basic tasks.
Second, we asked the controllers to perform our proposed
interactions and review the visual animations of how virtual
values change on a graphical user interface, in response to
interactions with the tangibles. Third, we showed the con-
trollers a short demonstration of how the tangible model could
autonomously move on the touch screen and asked if they
preferred an interface on which every aircraft is represented
by one tangible model, or if they liked to keep one model
in their hand and move the model manually on the interface.

We first tested our initial designs with 13 controllers, iterated
the design of all prototypes (which we explain in more detail
in the next section) and returned to the control center for a
second round of user tests. In the second session, 11 controllers,
who did not participate in the first round, tested all three
prototypes.

4.1.
Findings in relation to one-face-to-the-ATC

Based on the suggestions from our first user sample, we added
weather data and a webcam image of the runway to the right
panel and removed the visual representation of the flight strip.
Controllers no longer needed the strip, as long as all crucial
information was displayed and could be manipulated inside the
label (Controller 23, C21, C20, C19, C17, C16 and C13).

We identified two groups of controllers with opposing pref-
erences regarding the label. One group requested a reduced
label, where they could, for example, ‘directly manipulate
values in the upper line’ (C24) and had ‘minimalistic labels
of other aircraft’ that are not within their responsibility (C13).
The other group appreciated the availability of detailed visual
information inside the label and assistive systems. To fulfill
both needs, we decided to implement an extended label, which
holds further information and allows interactions for each
aircraft (Fig. 3) but is only displayed for the aircraft in focus
(i.e. selected with the dial or tangible, or hovered with the
mouse cursor). The extended labels include information that
had formerly been present in the flight strip or broadcasted
by aircraft via so-called mode S. Mode S displays the actual
instrument adjustments made by the pilot, such as flight level
or speed. Mode S data enable the controller to see the changes
made by a pilot and are frequently used by approach con-
trollers in their current system. In the second design itera-
tion, we added the aircraft type, expected runway, as well as
weather information and atmospheric pressure to the extended
label.

Finally, controllers appreciated the effort to display distance
vectors between aircraft. Seven controllers even suggested
spanning vectors by drawing a line from one target to another
because this interaction is more direct (C23, C16–21).

4.2.
Findings in relation to mouse interaction

Controllers considered the scrolling interaction to adjust values
as intuitive (C20 and C3) or quickly became accustomed to
scrolling (C23). C12 liked the suggestion of common clear-
ances, and C19 even claimed that with the mouse interaction
he ‘could handle much more traffic than usual’. Furthermore,
controllers preferred single clicks for defaults, such as sending
an aircraft to the next controller (C22 and C3) or holding (C4).
To interact even more efficiently, certain controllers envisioned
shortcuts. For the future, C4 imagined just pushing a fourth
mouse button to confirm all suggested values and sending them
to the aircraft via so-called data link communications (Bone

and Long, 2016). Data link is a text-based system to transmit
information directly to the aircraft’s cockpit, which therefore
eliminates the need for a physical switch to engage radio com-
munication (Gürlük et al., 2014). C22 and C20 realized that
scrolling might take too long when suggested values needed
much adjustment. C14 and C20 reminded us that the actual
work takes place in the controller’s head, and they, therefore,
‘want to spend as little time as possible on input’ (C20)—
‘except when our workload would be relieved by dropping the
radio communication’ (C14).

4.3.
Findings in relation to the flight dial

The majority of controllers intuitively understood the operation
with the dial. Notably controllers appreciated the documenta-
tion of individual values with the dial (C24 and C21) and its
haptic feedback (C13). The greatest criticism concerned the
‘long distances’ that have to be covered with the dial (C21,
C19 and C14). Scrolling to specific values and confirming
them, as well as leaving the context menu to select aircraft
via the dial (C16), involved too many rotations. Therefore, we
implemented a button to the right of the label (Fig. 3) to confirm
all adjusted and suggested values in the second iteration. To
select individual aircraft, most controllers preferred the touch
screen, because it is ‘more efficient’ especially in ‘high traffic
situations’ (C17 and C18). In the second evaluation session,
the values were sufficiently realistic for the controllers to
accept the new interface and they provided advice in relation
to fine tuning of its interaction performance. C19, C18 and
C17 mentioned that the ILS clearance usually follows a certain
heading clearance. Therefore, we implemented an ILS button
(Fig. 3), which provides a context sensitive shortcut to the ILS
and reduces the scrolling distance.

4.4.
Findings in relation to the tangible models

Regarding the shape of the models, the majority of controllers
in the first session preferred larger models—‘the larger
the easier’ (C11)—especially for the whale and flatterer
(Fig. 5c and d, respectively). Therefore, we increased the size
of the models significantly and printed larger 3D versions
for the second user test, in which all controllers preferred
the larger versions (Fig. 5e). In the first iteration, 7 out of 13
controllers had favored the shape of the flatterer, because of its
ergonomic grip and resemblance of a computer mouse. There
were different opinions as to whether the models should be
usable in all directions or have a unique orientation. In addition,
many controllers suggested customizable models with different
sizes and shapes.

The controllers agreed that several models moving across
the screen were too confusing, too ‘playful’ and, therefore,
unnecessary. They agreed to hold the models in their hand
while working and to place them in the appropriate position,
when needed. Another suggestion was to redundantly display

the interface on a large screen for observation and a small
screen for manipulation, as already tested in a study by
Alonso et al. (2007). For this type of interaction, the tangible
should be easy to hold without effort, especially over longer
periods. Furthermore, the participants preferred a solid
standing tangible with a rough surface for a better grip. The
controllers requested ergonomic shapes that clearly signaled
their affordance in terms of interaction.

In terms of advantages and disadvantages, the participants
had different opinions. C24 claimed to ‘work faster with
tangible models’, because ‘all interactions can be performed
with one input device’. C10 added that tangible models ‘allow
multiple inputs and can replace the step of opening a menu’,
whereas C8 expected only ‘limited time saving’ while working,
by comparison with other input devices. C10 in contrast
assumed inputs, such as speed or flight level to be ‘insanely
fast’. The majority expected to work faster with tangible
models.

The controllers disagreed about the balance between fun
while working and ease of use. C10 noted that controllers ‘need
to get used to’ the model interaction because they are ‘new
and small’, and C20 was afraid to cover too much screen and
thus miss important information. This showed that the size
of the models is a critical component, because they cannot
be too small for interaction but might hide important infor-
mation if they are too big. The majority could not imagine
working without a pen for its crucial function of facilitating
handwriting but liked the innovative models and enjoyed using
them.

C8 stated that everyone could handle the tangible models
because they offered an ‘intuitive approach’. Even C6, who
generally disliked the tangible models, considered the haptic
feedback a good idea. On the other hand, many controllers
raised concerns about the precision of use. C2 suspected that
working with the models would not pose a problem with a
little practice. C17 even claimed to be able to ‘work blind’
with the tangibles and that ‘stressful situations would not be a
problem either’. Despite an intuitive use of the tangible models,
the majority had concerns about the required precision and
dexterity. C19 remarked that using the models feels ‘buggy’
and C18 worried about ‘missing dexterity’.

The controllers had different ideas about the performance
of basic interactions with the models. The following list (for
a visual representation see Fig. 6) summarizes the interactions
that controllers mentioned several times in relation to the
typical approach tasks:

• Identify aircraft by touching the interface (especially the
label) with the model
• Document a new heading by rotating the model
• Document a lower flight level by tilting the model for-
ward or pushing it down
• Document a slower speed by sliding the model against
the flight direction or tilting it backward

5.
SUMMATIVE EVALUATION

Following the formative evaluations, we iterated the proto-
types and conducted a summative evaluation. The aim of the
summative evaluation was to test whether the prototypes that
emerged from the design process provided a better UX in
a standard scenario than the controllers’ current system. For
better comparability, we reduced the prototypes to minimalistic
default settings without visual aids or assistive systems that
only few controllers would activate.

5.1.
Method

5.1.1. Participants
To find a large effect (repeated-measure ANOVA, Cohen’s
f = 0.5, α = 0.05 and β = 0.85), we would have needed at least
14 participants (G∗Power; Faul et al., 2007). However, because
controllers are a scarce user group, we were limited to a sample
of N = 12 participants for this study. All experienced approach
controllers were male and participated during their shift. Three
of them had not been in touch with the research project before.
All participants provided informed consent.

5.1.2. Design
The experiment used a within-subjects design with the con-
ditions mouse, tangible, dial and current workstation (CW).
Because we had only access to a limited number of participants,
half of the controllers completed the conditions in the order
mouse > tangible > dial > CW and the other half experienced
the reverse order. In the conditions mouse, tangible, and dial
the controllers engaged with the respective prototype until the
scenario was completed. In the CW condition, we asked partic-
ipants to recall the current system they work with every day.

As dependent variables, we measured UX with the short ver-
sion of the User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ-S; Schrepp
et al., 2017) and the motivational orientation with the Hedonic
and Eudaimonic Motives for Activities (HEMA; Huta, 2016).
Additionally, we used the scales for competency and autonomy
from Sheldon’s psychological need inventory (Sheldon et al.,
2001), which we considered most appropriate for the domain
of ATC. All scales describe aspects of UX and therefore,
higher ratings on the scales indicate higher UX. Since our
designs aimed at improving UX for ATC, we expected the novel
prototypes to produce higher ratings on all scales compared to
the CW.

5.1.3. Material
We set up two workstations with the prototypes in a large
conference room. The flight dial prototype ran on a 27′′ touch-
screen and could be operated by both touch and the dial, as
described in section 3.2. The mouse prototype was presented
on a standard 24′′ desktop monitor and could be operated with
a three-button wireless mouse. To avoid lag in a live Wizard-
of-Oz performance, we pre-recorded a video with the tangible

prototype displaying all necessary interactions to complete the
scenario. We presented the video on the monitor together with
the flatterer as a physical prop.

5.1.4. Tasks
To experience all prototypes in a realistic traffic scenario, we
presented three aircraft that were already in the controller’s
area of responsibility and currently lined up on the so-called
‘downwind’, that is, moving in parallel but in the opposite
direction to the final approach. In this situation, the feeder
needs to reduce speed and altitude, turn each aircraft onto the
final and establish them on the ILS before handing them over
to the tower control. In detail, the minimal clearances required
until the first aircraft could be handed over to the tower control
to land on runway 26R were as follows:

• Identify all aircraft (i.e. acknowledge responsibility)
• Ask aircraft to descend to 4000 ft
• Reduce aircraft speed to 200 knots or less
• Adjust aircraft heading first to 170
• Then adjust aircraft heading to 230 or 240 and clear them
for the ILS

Before conducting the study, we validated the traffic scenario
with one air traffic controller who did not participate in the
evaluation.

5.1.5. Procedure
Upon arrival in the conference room, we explained to the
controllers the general scope of the research project and what
was expected from them in the study. After they signed the
consent form, we explained the layout of the labels in all
prototypes.

First, for each of the three conditions that involved novel
prototypes, we explained the controllers the features of the
interaction concept and the limitations of the prototype (e.g.
our prototypes focused on the interaction concept, rather than
the realistic aircraft behavior, therefore, aircraft did not fly a
smooth curve when turning but after a five second delay, turned
at the instructed angle). We asked controllers to try the features
as we explained them.

Second, when the controllers had no further questions, we
asked controllers to follow the scenario of guiding all aircraft
toward the final until at least one aircraft was established on
the ILS and handed over to the tower controller. In the tangible
condition, we instructed controllers to watch the video until the
end and then skip between the scenes in the video that they
wished to see again until they entirely understood the prototype
and were able to operate it. We encouraged the controllers to
mimic the clearly depicted interactions in the video with the
tangible prop provided.

Third, after the controllers completed the scenario and com-
municated that they had finished exploring the features, we
asked them to complete a questionnaire. We instructed the

controllers to think of the holistic interaction with the respective
prototype or the CW when answering the questionnaire, such
as the input and output opportunities and how these felt, rather
than minor details such as the font or colors. For the CW
condition, we instructed participants to recall the interaction
with their current system before filling in the questionnaire.
The participants had a schematic picture (Fig. 1) as memory
aid, while recalling the interaction. Additionally, the evaluation
took place between their shifts, so their memory was fresh.
We invited controllers to comment on the prototypes when
appropriate during the experiment.

6.
RESULTS

We calculated repeated-measure ANOVAs of each dependent
variable with planned contrasts between each prototype and
the CW. We summarize the results below and provide a visual
representation of the results in Fig. 7. The complete descriptive
and inferential statistical results are provided in the appendix,
Table 1.

Despite the small sample of N = 12 controllers, there were no
outliers with z > 3. Data met the assumption of sphericity on all
but the eudaimonia scale, for which we used the Greenhouse–
Geisser correction. Based on a Shapiro–Wilk test, the data were
normally distributed except for the differences in the compe-
tence scale between the CW and the dial (P < 0.001), and the
CW and the mouse (P = 0.018). However, non-parametrical
tests led to the same results regarding statistical significance
as the parametrical tests; therefore, we present parametrical
results for reasons of consistency with all other data. Due to
the scarcity of our user group, we had to conduct our summative
evaluation with fewer participants than the conservative power
analysis suggested. Following this limitation of feasibility to
sample size (Caine, 2016) that only allowed for an underpow-
ered study, we focus on effect sizes rather than p-values. We
considered ηp
2 > .14 to indicate large effects.
The repeated-measure ANOVAs with contrasts indicated
that all three prototypes had higher overall UEQ-S scores than
the CW. This is mainly due to the high values on the hedonic
sub-scale because, on the pragmatic sub-scale, the controllers
only rated the mouse prototype significantly higher than the
CW. Similarly, the hedonic scale of the HEMA showed a sig-
nificant large difference between each prototype and the CW.
Neither the eudemonia scale of the HEMA nor the autonomy
scale for needs indicated any significant differences. Against
our hypothesis, the data indicated that the CW gives controllers
a greater satisfaction in terms of their psychological need for
competence than the tangible prototype.

7.
DISCUSSION

As hypothesized, the summative evaluation revealed that UX is
higher with the novel prototypes than with the CW currently in
use. The mouse interface surpassed all other interfaces descrip-

tively on the pragmatic scale. However, the medium effect
size (ηp
2 = .101, see supplementary Table 1) between dial and
CW signals that also the dial interface has pragmatic potential.
Especially the more original interaction concepts, such as the
dial and tangible models had descriptively high values on
the hedonic subscale of the UEQ-S that can be considered
excellent, compared to international benchmarks (Hinderks et
al., 2018). In the UEQ-S, the hedonic quality consists of the
aspects of stimulation and novelty (Schrepp et al., 2017). Using
unfamiliar interaction concepts in a prototype, the controllers
merely experienced explains part of the perceived novelty of the
dial and tangibles. However, the hedonic motives scale, which
does not explicitly contain novelty but is based on ‘seeking
pleasure and comfort’ (Huta and Ryan, 2010), shows a similar
pattern to the UEQ-S hedonic subscale. Together with the
benchmark ranking of the UEQ-S, we therefore conclude that
all three prototypes truly created a more pleasurable, stimulat-
ing experience that exceeded the novelty of the interaction.

On the scale for eudaimonic orientation, there was no
significant main effect or contrasts; however, the difference
between the dial and the CW can be considered as a large
effect (ηp
2 = .163, see supplement Table 1). This is particularly
interesting, because the concept of eudaimonia is associated

with long-term importance (Mekler and Hornbæk, 2016). Post-
hoc, we wondered how the entirely novel prototypes, only
seen once before by some controllers, could surpass the CW
used to solve standard and non-standard situations on a daily
basis. One possible explanation is that the task itself sparks
meaning, and the ability to directly manipulate data associated
with the task, increased the ratings of eudaimonia. This is
supported by significant correlations of the eudaimonic motive
with the pragmatic experience of the UEQ-S for the mouse
interface (r = 0.84, P < 0.001), the dial (r = 0.76, P = 0.005)
and the tangibles (r = 0.58, P = 0.046). This correlation and
interpretation are in line with Mekler and Hornbæk (2016)
who suggested that the high pragmatic quality of technology
facilitated users’ striving toward personal goods. The lack of
correlation between the eudaimonic motive and the pragmatic
experience for the CW (r = 0.25, P = 0.43) indicates that
controllers’ eudaimonic motives for the system with which they
are already familiar, involve more factors than pragmatic UX.

Additionally, in our data the overall ratings for eudaimonia
and hedonia are comparatively high, with descriptive statistics
for eudaimonia being above the scores published by Mekler and
Hornbæk (2016) and hedonia below their scores. Our data do
not support the idea of Grundgeiger et al. (2020), who argued

that eudaimonic motives and related needs such as competence
and autonomy may be more important than hedonia in safety-
critical domains. One reason for the unexpected results of
the eudaimonic motives scale is a potential confound between
conditions. While measuring eudaimonia on the state-level in
all four conditions (Huta and Waterman, 2014), the period
of time in the three prototype conditions was limited to the
short interaction experience, whereas there was no limitation
for the period of time in which controllers reminisced on CW
experiences.

The controllers rating of satisfied need for competence when
using the novel prototypes, was lower than with the CW. How-
ever, data from the contextual inquiry indicates that controllers
needed time and training to achieve today’s levels of speed
and proficiency (Huber et al., 2020). Controllers in our study
had not used the prototypes in their final layout before the
summative evaluation. Dulchinos (2018) showed that few hours
of training with a new system can suffice for controllers to
perceive their performance as higher than with the old system.

We asked controllers for their experience when interacting
with the system, but we cannot distinguish from our data
to what degree the new visualizations influenced controllers’
rating. However, due to the safety and efficiency focus of
controllers’ training, we would argue that the interactions had
a larger influence on their satisfaction than visual esthetics of
the interface. This aspect needs to be covered in future work.

9.
LIMITATIONS

There are several limitations to our work. First, we need to point
out that the contextual inquiry in the initial underlying user
research (Huber et al., 2020) as well as our formative evalu-

ations, focused on approach controllers in the feeder position
and their co-workers. This limits the generalizability of result-
ing data for controllers in other positions. We concentrated
on the interaction with the system by an individual controller
during the design process. We suspect that our interaction
concepts can also support the collaborative work of controllers
with colleagues and pilots. However, we have not explicitly
designed and tested collaborative work scenarios, which should
be addressed in future work.

Second, the current workstation under study also poses limi-
tations. For legal reasons, controllers are currently obliged to
document every instruction to the pilot. This documentation
involves additional interaction beyond the task itself. Despite
limitations to our design decisions, we decided to include this
requirement in our design process to achieve better acceptance
during the user tests. Furthermore, we designed ambidextrous
interactions even though today, approach controllers need to
operate the radio with one hand. If this constraint does not cease
to exist with the introduction of data link communications in
the approach position, the input devices will be limited to one-
handed use or will require alternatives, such as a foot pedal,
a transmission button embedded in the primary interaction
device, or an always-on microphone to replace the separate
handheld push-to-talk button.

Third, our summative evaluation focused on quantifying UX.
All reported and discussed cues on how the prototypes perform
in terms of efficiency, are based on controllers’ qualitative
assessments. Future work needs to investigate whether the
new concepts allow for safe and efficient operation and still
hold up UX over time. Unlike analyses regarding the trade-off
between efficiency and thoroughness (Takagi and Nakanishi,
2020) or safety (Karikawa et al., 2014), the UX of controllers
operating a system cannot easily be determined for each single
interaction or decision. Instead, more holistic measures for UX
over a task sequence or an entire shift should be compared
to cumulated operationalizations of safety and efficiency. The
increased pressure in a full-scale high-fidelity simulation may
decrease hedonic qualities of use.

Fourth, even though the selected use cases could be managed
with all systems, the interactivity of prototypes in the study
varied. For the tangible prototype, air traffic controllers did not
experience the interaction in the summative evaluation but held
a tangible prop to perform actions while watching the video.
However, controllers had experienced the tangible interaction
in the formative evaluations. Possible implementations of the
prototype in future work may be possible using magnetic bits
for near-surface interaction (Liang et al., 2013) and deformable
interfaces (Rossmy, 2020). When exploring prototypes with
higher fidelity, safety aspects such as the detectability of system
status and systems’ behavior in the case of failure require
consideration.

Fifth, we grounded the summative evaluation of interac-
tion concepts exclusively on success cases and controllers’
most frequent interactions. Their current workstations obvi-

ously exceed this minimalistic feature set for the price of
less simplicity in the user interface. Hence, the controllers’
recollection of the additional functionality and complexity of
their current workstation may have caused an underestimation
of UX. The next step would be to embed the interactions in
an interface encompassing the features and information that
facilitate controllers to deal with different situations instead
of just the most frequent sequences. When envisioning these
interfaces, safety assurance should play a major role. Safety
criteria need to be derived to define what is considered safe
in relation to the changed interface. This will allow to define
safety objectives and to assess in simulation studies whether
these safety objectives are satisfied.

Sixth, this work is based on insights from contextual
inquiries and follows the entire contextual design process as
suggested by Holtzblatt and Beyer (2016). We acknowledge
that other experience-centered design processes exist, such as
the experience categories method proposed by Zeiner et al.
(2018) or methods based on activity theory (Savioja et al.,
2014; Roto et al., 2017). In future work, alternative methods
may reveal the whole spectrum of UX in ATC to broaden
designers’ understanding of controllers’ experiences.

10.
CONCLUSION

In this work, we applied a user-centered design process to
the context of ATC to improve controllers’ UX. We extracted
controllers’ key user needs regarding interaction and developed
a unified view of the ATC workplace with three prototypes,
each using a different input method and interaction concept.
Apart from proposing three interaction concepts, the main
contribution of this work is to quantify UX dimensions in
the context of ATC. In a summative evaluation, all prototypes
surpassed the existing system in terms of hedonic quality,
and single prototypes indicated higher values for eudaimonic
motives or pragmatic experiences. The great leap in UX can
encourage designers in other safety-critical domains to think
beyond safety and efficiency.

Despite the varying degree of novelty among the mouse,
dial and tangibles, we were able to derive general implications
concerning the design of future ATC systems from qualitative
data, gathered in user tests. Insights include recommendations
on using a minimalistic design and facilitating efficient interac-
tions, as well as customizable interface elements. These find-
ings can be applied in future ATC interface design, regardless of
implemented interaction technology. Thereby we investigated
the holistic interaction experience—determining the role of
visual esthetics in ATC interface design is up for future work.
We contributed three exemplary interaction concepts, catering
for user needs and positive experiences. However, a shift in
future design projects in safety-critical environments, such as
ATC is necessary to think beyond developing prototypes specif-
ically for safety and efficiency. Consequently, when putting UX
first in early design stages, subsequent safety impact analyses

are necessary to ensure the novel interface will promote the
workplace priorities, safety, structure and efficiency instead of
causing a tradeoff between positive experiences and safety.

In addition, we suggest designing for meaningful interac-
tions and experiences of familiarity, trust, engagement and joy.
This could be achieved, for instance with an interaction device
that combines the precision and hedonic aspects of the dial with
the increased degrees of freedom of the mouse. Future work
needs to investigate, how engaging interfaces maintaining vig-
ilance in periods of low activity and efficient interfaces during
high traffic situations can be molded within a single system.
