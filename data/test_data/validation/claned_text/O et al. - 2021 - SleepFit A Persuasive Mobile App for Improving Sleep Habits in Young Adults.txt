Abstract—Sleep disorders have been associated with mental 
distress, depressive symptoms, and anxiety. Digital interventions 
using mobile technology can address sleep difficulties due to the 
pervasiveness of smartphones, especially among young adults. 
Therefore, we design a persuasive mobile app, called SleepFit, 
targeted at young adults with the aim of improving their sleep 
habits or behaviours which in turn would improve their mental 
health and wellbeing. To achieve this, we employ the user-centered 
design approach in five stages. First, we elicit user preferences by 
conducting two concurrent focus group (FG) sessions to 
understand participants’ current sleep habits, contributing 
factors, and how a persuasive mobile app (such as SleepFit) can 
help to improve their situation. Second, we design low-fidelity 
prototypes (LFP) illustrating various features of SleepFit based on 
our findings from the FG sessions after data analysis. Third, we 
conduct a user study to assess perceived persuasiveness of the 
features illustrated by the LFP. Our findings show that users 
perceived 7 features (Sleep Analysis, News Feed, Smart Alarm, 
Chat, Sleep Music, Notification, and Sleep Diary) as significantly 
persuasive for improving their sleep habits. Fourth, we design 
high-fidelity prototypes which reflect only the features that are 
perceived as significantly persuasive, based on the results of the 
LFP evaluation. Finally, we conduct a usability evaluation of the 
high-fidelity prototypes (HFP) and then refine the HFP based on 
qualitative feedback after thematic analysis. 

Keywords—sleep disorder, mental health, persuasive technology, 
persuasive strategies, mobile app, behaviour change, user-centered 
design, thematic analysis, wellbeing 

I. INTRODUCTION 

Mental health disorders (MHDs) are on the rise globally. 
According to the World Health Organization, 1 in 4 people are 
affected by MHDs and 3 in 4 people with severe MHDs are 
untreated [1]. Depression, which is an MHD, continues to be 
prevalent among young adults [2] and has been projected to be 
the leading cause of disease burden globally by 2030 [1]. In 
addition, suicide is already the second cause of death among 
young adults [1]. 

Despite the large burden imposed by MHDs on health 
systems globally, addressing the risk factors of MHD is one of 

the effective ways of curtailing its spread. Research has shown 
that lack of quality sleep (also called sleep disorders) causes 
psychotic experiences (such as hallucinations and paranoia) and 
other mental health problems [3]. Sleep disorders (such as 
insomnia, dyssomnia or sleep insufficiency) have also been 
associated with mental distress, depressive symptoms and 
anxiety [4], [5]. Sleep disorders are prevalent among young 
adults [6]–[9], hence the need to design interventions that can 
help to improve their sleep behaviours or habits. 

Digital interventions using mobile technology will be a 
better approach due to the pervasiveness of smartphones, 
especially among young adults. For example, more than 90% of 
young adults own a smartphone in developed countries, while at 
most 85% of young adults use a smartphone in developing 
countries [10]. Therefore, interventions in form of mobile health 
(mHealth) apps that improve sleep habits can yield a positive 
outcome for young adults experiencing sleep difficulties. 

In this paper, we design a mHealth app, called SleepFit, for 
improving sleep behaviours or habits in young adults which in 
turn will improve their mental health and wellbeing. To achieve 
our goal, we employed the user-centered design approach [11], 
[12] for app design and evaluation, as described in the following 
five stages: 

1. We elicited user preferences by conducting two 
concurrent focus group sessions. The objective of the 
sessions is to understand users’ current sleep pattern, 
how they manage it using mHealth apps, and the 
features they prefer in those apps or any other apps that 
aim to improve sleep behaviours (such as SleepFit). The 
focus group (FG) method allowed us to gather broad and 
diverse perspectives from participants with different 
levels of sleep disturbance, and ultimately provided 
insights and ideas on what features will be required to 
improve sleep behaviours or habits in young adults. 

2. We designed low-fidelity prototypes (LFP) illustrating 
various features based on the qualitative feedback and 
suggestions obtained via the FG sessions.

3. We conducted a user study to assess the perceived 
persuasiveness of the features in the LFP design. The 
perceived persuasiveness scale [13] measures each 
feature’s perceived effectiveness in influencing users’ 
sleep habits. Our findings showed that users perceived 
7 features (Sleep Analysis, News Feed, Smart Alarm,

4. Based on the results of the LFP evaluation, we designed

5. After the HFP evaluation, we refined the high-fidelity 
prototypes based on qualitative feedback after thematic 
analysis. 

Finally, we reflected upon our findings and discussed design 
implications. 

II. RELATED WORK 
Previous works on sleep tracking or monitoring through 
mobile apps adopt the manual, automated or hybrid techniques 
in achieving their objectives. For example, Min et al. [14] 
developed a sleep tracking app, called Toss ‘N’ Turn (TNT). 
The goal of this app is to collect device information required to 
detect sleep and wake states, as well as daily and global sleep 
quality. TNT used phone sensors to collect information such as 
sound amplitude (using microphone), light intensity (using 
ambient 
light 
sensor), 
and 
movement/motion 
(using 
accelerometer). It also collected screen proximity sensor values 
and device states (e.g., screen on/off, battery-charging state, and 
running apps). In addition to sensor readings, TNT has a sleep 
diary feature that allows users to provide information related to 
their sleep patterns, such as sleep time, wake-up time, subjective 
sleep quality rating (from poor to very good), sleep disturbances, 
phone location during sleep, co-sleeping behaviour (whether 
sleeping alone, or with someone else), etc.  

Similarly, Krishna et al. [15] developed a sleep tracker and 
sleep duration estimator app, called SleepSensei. The app tracks 
user’s movements (using accelerometer) within a 2-minute 
window after generating an auditory tone for 5 seconds every 15 
minutes. This approach allows the authors to determine sleep 
state. They also account for sleep interruptions by providing 
additional features to collect phone usage information (such as 
phone calls, messages, etc.) during sleep time. Environmental 
data such as weather conditions (e.g., temperature, humidity, 
rainfall, etc.), ambient sound (e.g., music and noise) and light 
intensity are also collected. The authors analyzed these data and 
used it to predict wake-up time which, in turn, triggers an alarm. 

Furthermore, Choe et al. [16] aim to reduce the capture 
burden of manual sleep tracking and improve access to 
information. They leveraged optional widgets on both lock 
screen and home screen to expose features within their app, 
called SleepTight. Thus, users can quickly access key features, 
such as daily sleep diary and add activity with a single tab 
without unlocking the screen or launching the app from the 
phone’s desktop. SleepTight allows users to capture sleep 
behaviours (such as sleep quality, sleep duration, etc.) and 
contributing factors (such as meals, caffeine, tobacco, alcohol, 

medication, etc.) through the sleep diary feature. The app also 
allows users to visualize their sleep duration and quality, as well 
as make comparisons in relation to the contributing factors. 
While Shirazi et al. [17] did not use widgets for their 
Somnometer app, they provided a feature that allows users to 
share sleep information with their friends on Facebook. 

The gap in the related works described above is that the 
highlighted app features were based on designer’s intuition 
rather than on users’ preferences and perceived persuasiveness 
of the features. Our work addressed this gap by involving target 
users throughout the design process and evaluating the features 
from a persuasion standpoint. First, we understand users’ sleep 
habits and elicit their preferred features, and then evaluated these 
features to assess their perceived persuasiveness. We employed 
features that were perceived as significantly persuasive (i.e., 
able to motivate or influence users to change their unhealthy 
sleep habits/behaviours) in the final app design. 

III. METHOD 
To achieve our goal, we employ a user-centered design 
approach that involves conducting focus group (FG) sessions to 
elicit user preferences, followed by low-fidelity prototype 
design and evaluation, and then high-fidelity prototype design 
and evaluation. 

A. Study 1: Focus Group 
To understand our target audience, we conducted two focus 
group sessions. This allowed us to gather broad and diverse 
perspectives from participants with respect to their sleep habits 
such as sleep duration, bedtime, wakeup time, sleep disturbance 
and contributing factors, etc. Research has shown that the type 
and range of data generated through the social interaction of 
focus groups are often deeper and richer than those obtained 
from one-to-one interviews [18], [19]. 10 participants within the 
age group of our target audience (17-35 years) were recruited to 
participate in the study. The participants were assigned to two 
groups: FG1 (n=5) and FG2 (n=5). The two sessions ran for at 
least 35 minutes each with a moderator and assistant facilitating 
each session. The moderators ran the study by using a list of 
questions to facilitate discussion among participants, while the 
assistant moderators took notes and audio-recorded the sessions. 
The focus group sessions were conducted in two stages, as 
follows: 

 Introduction: The moderator and assistant for each 
session introduced themselves to the participants, 
described the primary goal of the focus group, and gave 
a brief explanation of sleep disorders. Afterwards, the 
participants were asked to introduce themselves by their 
first name only. 

 Group discussion: Participants were allowed to interact 
with each other during the sessions in response to various 
questions raised by the moderators, such as their daily 
sleep time (bedtime and wakeup time), how they 
currently monitor or track their sleep (e.g., use of mobile 
apps or alarm clock), factors affecting their sleep quality, 
preferred features in a mobile app that can improve their 
sleep quality, etc. 

 

1) Data Analysis and Results 
To analyze the data, we transcribed audio recordings from 
the focus group sessions into texts (after listening to each 
recording). Next, we conducted thematic analysis of the 
transcripts and the notes taken during the sessions to extract 
themes that represent user preferences. This informed the design 
of the LFP. 

Participants’ daily sleep duration ranges from 2 hours to 8 
hours, with an average of 6.1 hours. Also, participants 
highlighted that their sleep quality is often affected negatively 
by the following factors: academics (assignments, projects, 
deadlines), social media (Facebook, WhatsApp, etc.), social life 
(parties, meeting with friends), light, noise, and stress. 

Participants suggested certain features that can improve their 
sleep quality, such as sleep music, cool visualizations, 
notification/reminder, bedtime recommendation, sleep tracking, 
setting sleep goal/duration, and virtual rewards. Participants also 
discussed their perceptions and views about these features, 
including those suggested by the moderators (based on literature 
review). Results of thematic analysis of the qualitative data 
collected uncovered the following themes: (1) analysis and 
visualization, (2) bedtime and wake-up time suggestion, and (3) 
sleep improvement using specific features (such as sleep diary, 
sleep music, automated tracking, notification/reminder, etc.). In 
summary, below are the preferred features based on the FG 
sessions. 

a) Smart Alarm: 60% of the participants liked the idea of 
an alarm that automatically determines the best wake-up time 
based on their sleep patterns, and they agreed to use it and also 
want to be able to configure it (e.g., to turn it off when not 
required). 

b) Sleep Analysis: 100% of the participants were 
interested to visually see their progress over time using graphs 
or charts. Sample comments include: “I will like to see how 
many hours I sleep” [P7], “It should show visualizations per 
week.” [P8]. 

c) Sleep Diary: 80% of the participants found this feature 
useful and agreed to use it in the sleep app to log their daily 
wake-up time, bedtime, as well as factors affecting their sleep 
patterns and sleep quality. 

d) Automated Tracking: 80% of the participants prefer 
this feature. However, some participants were concerned about 
the level of data that would be collected through the phone 
sensors and if there is an option to disable automated tracking.  

e) News Feed: 100% of the participants liked the idea of 
a feature that can offer personalized sleep-related news, 
tutorials or tips based on their sleep patterns and quality.  

f) Notification: 80% of the participants liked the idea of 
notification or reminder in the app. Some participants want the 
flexibility to turn it off or on (e.g., “Be able to customize it.” 
[P10]). 

g) Chat: 60% of the participants liked the idea of being 
able to chat with a sleep specialist in case of sleep-related issues 
or whenever expert advice is required on how to handle sleep 
disorders.  

h) Sleep Music: 20% of the participants suggested “sleep 
music” as a potential feature, as shown in the following 

comments: “Maybe some Sleep Music which can help me fall 
asleep.” [P3], “Music will be good feature.” [P9]. 

The features above, including Virtual Rewards which was 
suggested by 40% of participants, became the potential features 
of the SleepFit app. In Study 2, we evaluated these features, 
except “Automated Tracking” which does not require user 
interaction (since it runs in the background and collects data that 
can be visualized via the Sleep Analysis feature), to assess their 
perceived persuasiveness.

their perceived persuasiveness. Fig. 1 shows the LFP illustrating

We evaluated the LFP through an online survey. We 
presented the prototype illustrating each feature to the 
participants as a set of images arranged in a fashion depicting 

typical user interaction with the feature. Each prototype is 
immediately followed by a set of questions assessing the 
perceived persuasiveness of the feature. The questions were 
based on a validated scale adapted from [13] and used in many 
persuasive technology research including [21]–[25]. The 
questions, which were measured on a 5-point Likert scale 
(ranging from “1 - Strongly Disagree” and “5 - Strongly 
Agree”), are: (i) This feature would motivate me to adopt healthy 
sleep habits, (ii) This feature would convince or make me to 
reconsider my current sleep habits, and (iii) This feature would 
improve my current sleep habits. The survey also includes 
demographic questions such as age group, gender, marital status, 
employment status, etc., as well as open-ended questions aimed 
to collect participants’ qualitative feedback or suggestions 
related to the features presented. 

We included a total of 23 survey responses in our analysis 
after removing four incomplete responses. Our participants were 
young adults (17-35 years old) experiencing varying degrees of 
sleep disorders and also proficient in English. As regards 
demographic information, 4% of the participants were within 
17-21 age group, 57% were within 22-25 age group, 35% were 
within 26-30 age group, and 4% were within 31-35 age group. 
Moreover, 70% were males, while 30% were females. In 
addition, 87% were single, while 13% were married.  

1) Data Analysis and Results 
To analyze the data, we computed the average score (or 
persuasiveness score) for each feature, and then conducted a 
One-Sample t-test to determine the persuasiveness of the 
features with respect to the neutral score of 3 (based on a 5-point 
Likert scale). Next, to examine and compare the persuasiveness 
of the features, we performed a Repeated-Measure Analysis of 
Variance (RM-ANOVA) analysis with feature type as within 
subject factor (after validating for ANOVA assumptions), 
followed by pairwise comparison (using the Bonferroni method 
for adjusting degrees of freedom for multiple comparisons). 
When sphericity assumption was violated, we used the 
Greenhouse-Geisser method to correct the degrees of freedom. 

a) Perceived Persuasiveness of App Features: In general, 
the result of the One-Sample t-test showed that the 
persuasiveness scores of the features are significantly higher 
than the neutral score of 3 (p<.003), except Virtual Rewards 
(p<.144). This means that our participants perceived the 
features, except Virtual Rewards, as significantly persuasive. 
As shown in Fig. 2, Sleep Analysis is perceived as the most 
persuasive feature (Mean=4.116, SD=0.608), followed by

(Mean=3.899, SD=0.768), and Chat (Mean=3.870, SD=0.931). 
However, the Virtual Rewards feature is perceived as the least 
persuasive (Mean=3.290, SD=0.917). The rest of the features 
(Sleep Music, Notification, Sleep Diary) are in the middle. 
Furthermore, to determine if the features are significantly 
different in their persuasiveness overall, we conducted an RM-
ANOVA test. The results of the RM-ANOVA showed 
significant main effects of feature type on persuasiveness 
(F4.323, 95.102 = 3.210, p<.014). In other words, there is a 
significant difference between the features with respect to their 
persuasiveness overall. Specifically, the Bonferroni-corrected 

pairwise comparisons show that Sleep Analysis emerged as the 
most persuasive feature, significantly different from Sleep 
Diary (p<.003) and Virtual Rewards (p<.022). News Feed is the 
second most persuasive feature, significantly different from 
Virtual Rewards (p<.034) which emerged as the least 
persuasive.

C. Study 3: High-Fidelity Prototypes and Evaluation 
We created high-fidelity prototypes (HFP) illustrating the 7 
features that were perceived as significantly persuasive, based 
on the results of Study 2. In addition, we considered the 
qualitative feedback and suggestions offered by participants in 
the design of the HFP to ensure the SleepFit app is usable and 
acceptable to users. For example, we added a Community feature 
that support sharing of sleep ideas and experiences among peers 
within the app. The HFP was designed using the FluidUI 
prototyping tool [26]. We conducted a usability study through 
an online survey completed by 24 participants which were 
young adults with varying degrees of sleeping issues and also 
proficient in English.  

The online survey consists of two parts: demographic 
questions and usability questions. Table II shows the 
demographic information of participants. Participants were 
asked to either interact with the HFP on their smartphones (via 
the FluidUI mobile app) or watch a video showing a typical user 
interacting with the HFP (similar to the approach used by [27]), 
and then answer the usability questions. We embedded the video 
in the online survey to make it easily accessible to participants. 
The usability questions assess the app in terms of aesthetics 
(e.g., color, graphics, layout, etc.), ease of use, and overall user 
experience. In addition, participants were asked to offer 
suggestions on how the app could be improved. Fig. 3 shows the 
HFP of Sleep Diary, Sleep Analysis, and News Feed features. 
The findings from this study were used to refine the HFP.

IV. DISCUSSION 

In this paper, we designed a persuasive mobile app 
(SleepFit) that allows users to track their sleep patterns with 
respect to their goal, observe the factors affecting their sleep 
quality negatively, and receive personalized interventions that 
improve their sleep quality. Our design process is user-centered 
rather than designer-driven. This design approach led to the 
selection of features that users perceived as significantly 
persuasive in motivating or influencing the desired change in 
their sleep habits or behaviours, while the usability evaluation 
led to an app design that is usable and engaging. Research has 
shown that a design approach that considers the experience of 

users and their involvement in the design process would produce 
a system that is user-centered, effective, and acceptable in daily 
life [28]–[30]. As shown in Table IV, SleepFit’s persuasive 
features align with various persuasive strategies (or behaviour 
change strategies)  proposed in well-established and widely used 
persuasion frameworks, such as the Persuasive Systems Design 
(PSD) framework [31] and the Goal-setting theory [32].

Furthermore, research shows that technologies that leverage 
social influence can motivate and persuade users since people 
naturally respond in social ways [34]. SleepFit applies the 
persuasion dynamics of social influence through its Community 
feature. This aligns with the “normative influence” (peer 
pressure) persuasive strategy [31], [34] which is one of the top 
5 strategies employed in mental health apps [35]. Users believed 
that providing means to read (or share) sleep-related success 
stories and experiences from (or with) their peers would 

motivate them to change their unhealthy sleep habits (see the 
Social sharing theme in Table I). 

Although the sample size of the studies may not be sufficient 
to draw strong conclusions, the perceived effectiveness 
assessment has led to the design of an app that employed 
persuasive features for promoting healthy sleep habits among 
young adults, thereby improving their physical and mental 
wellbeing. Moreover, subsequent refinements applied to the 
HFP based on participants’ evaluation feedback would improve 
the app’s usability and overall user experience/engagement. 

V. CONCLUSION 

We designed a persuasive mobile app, called SleepFit, 
targeted at young adults to improve their sleep habits or 
behaviours. We applied the user-centered design approach by 
first conducting two focus group (FG) sessions to elicit user 
preferences, and then designed and evaluated a low-fidelity 
prototype based on the FG findings and identified features 
perceived as significantly persuasive. Our findings showed that 
users perceived 7 features (Sleep Analysis, News Feed, Smart 
Alarm, Chat, Sleep Music, Notification, and Sleep Diary) as 
significantly persuasive for improving their sleep behaviours or 
habits. Finally, we designed and evaluated high-fidelity 
prototypes (HFP) that employed these persuasive features. Our 
usability evaluation findings revealed that most users were 
satisfied with the app design and believed that SleepFit is 
aesthetically pleasing, easy to use, and provide good user 
experience. Moreover, the few suggestions made by users were 
used in refining the HFP to further enhance the app’s usability 
and user experience. 

Future work will implement or develop the SleepFit app 
based on the HFP and deploy it on both Android and iOS 
platforms, and then conduct a field study to assess the app’s 
actual effectiveness.
