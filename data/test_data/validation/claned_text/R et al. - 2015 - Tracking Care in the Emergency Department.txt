FEATURE AT A GLANCE:
Results are presented from the 
ED LaunchPoint design project, 
which received honorable 
mention for the 2013 Stanley 
H. Caplan User-Centered 
Product Design Award. This 
redesign focused on patients’ 
progression through their 
emergency department visits, 
emphasizing time-sensitive 
phases for increasing clinical 
safety. User observation, 
iterative user-centered design, 
and usability testing placed 
users at the core of this project. 
Close collaboration allowed 
for the identification of optimal 
work flows and the creation of a 
design tailored to users’ needs.

E

lectronic medical records (EMRs)	
support clinicians in their efficient 
information management and 
communication within the emergency 
department (ED) and are essential for 
providing high-quality clinical care. In many 
health-care facilities, particularly those 
with complex environments, EMR systems 
are perceived as an obstacle to delivering 
patient care. The complex environment 
of an ED can already seem out of control, 
with multiple and simultaneous tasks being 
required of the clinicians. These tasks are 
often technically difficult, must be executed 
at a fast pace, and fail to include clarification 
for next steps (Smith & Feied, 1999). 
Because of high levels of system and task 
complexity, this type of work environment 
provides a variety of challenges for managing 
patient care.
Complex environments, such as the ED, 
are characterized by the variety of items 
incorporated into the system, the degree of 
interaction or interdependence among those 
items, how frequently or rapidly the status 
of items and interactions changes, and the 
predictability of such changes (Campbell, 
1988; Kannapallil, Schauer, Cohen, & Patel, 
2011; Kieras & Polson, 1985; Xiao, Hunter, 
MacKenzie, & Jefferies, 1996). Based on the 
challenges of this complex environment, 
our team developed a software tracking 
board called ED LaunchPoint, which enables 
ED physicians to efficiently care for their 
patients.
Our team’s work on ED LaunchPoint, 
which received honorable mention for the 
2013 Stanley H. Caplan User-Centered 
Product Design Award, provided us with the 
opportunity to redesign the way clinicians 
interact with their ED tracking software. The 

software redesign demonstrates successful 
integration of the user-centered design 
(UCD) process within the development 
culture at Cerner Corporation. As an orga-
nization, we assembled a cross-functional, 
multidisciplinary team of clinical strategists, 
business analysts, software architects, inter-
action designers, and usability researchers to 
analyze key processes and identify opportu-
nities for improvement to EMR systems.
In this article, we outline our process and 
the insights we gained during the design 
of ED LaunchPoint. To manage patients 
more efficiently and streamline the clini-
cian’s work flow, ED LaunchPoint needed 
to provide advanced capabilities in a more 
standard, focused view of ED tracking while 
also serving as a launch point for all future 
advancements in emergency medicine 
application. Our objective was to provide 
physicians with an overview of patient care, 
starting with a review of pertinent current 
and historical information to determine a 
diagnosis, continuing through the initial 
treatment plan, and ending with the conclu-
sion of the patient encounter.

The Process

Our team used a UCD process that inte-
grated iterative design and evaluation during 
the product development phase, as depicted 
in Figure 1. During the design and devel-
opment process, we carried out usability 
activities to support understanding of the 
user through early user research, create a 
vision of the user’s work flow through itera-
tive design, develop a solution and verify 
the design through usability testing, and 
deliver the results as a refined user experi-
ence. However, the type of application being 
developed and the environment in which

the user will be working play a part in the extent and type of 
methods used to explore the users and their work flow.
Our first step toward understanding and specifying the 
context of use was reviewing the users, their tasks, and the 
environments of use for the existing application. This created 
a vision of the users’ goals through iterative design, and it was 
during this time that user requirements for the solution were 
developed in sufficient detail to drive the design. We then 
refined design iterations through usability research before 
integrating them back into the design. Finally, a design solu-
tion was developed and validated to ensure that the users’ 
goals were accounted for within the design.

Understanding the Users

The development team for ED LaunchPoint considered 
the direct input of end users throughout the design. During 
the process of creating a vision and developing the solution, 
on-site visits and iterative usability testing provided clinical 
perspectives and verification of the specific clinical tasks being 
explored. Furthermore, the end users may encounter inter-
ruptions and several different task scenarios throughout a 
workday, which often includes frequently changing agendas 

during a single patient work flow (Westbrook et al., 2010). 
Working with the users during early stages of the develop-
ment process to understand the information needs within 
different task scenarios and with frequent interruptions was 
critical in supporting a good user experience.

Early User Research

On-site visits typically involved observing and inter-
viewing three or more health-care providers and nurses 
along with managers and directors in various departments. 
We performed early user research with 72 ED physicians 
across the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, and 
Australia. The chosen facilities, both academic and nonaca-
demic, ranged from community hospitals to large networks 
known as integrated delivery networks (IDNs). Utilizing the 
interviews, usage metrics, and on-site observation data, we 
focused on the system’s overall usability using metrics such as 
steps within the software that were required for completing a 
task, errors, user comments, and feedback regarding existing 
“pain points.”

project’s concept generation phase produced insights that

Creating a Vision

Creating a vision is the process of capturing, communi-
cating, and translating different user goals and organizing 
them into a cohesive whole. Once a shared vision for each 
concept was identified, the team focused on each goal and 
defined methods for achieving those goals. Techniques such 
as site maps and storyboards were crucial for identifying 
options that could be reviewed with the users. Our team 
sketched out each goal within the user’s work flow and 
produced iterations until we identified a draft that incorpo-
rated the best set of characteristics for meeting each user’s 
goals. A set of sketches created to illustrate iterations of the 

individual elements tracked for a single patient can be seen in 
Figure 2.
Storyboarding work flows on how users work through a 
plan of action enabled us to identify areas for improving the 
effectiveness of both tracking and placing orders throughout 
the duration of patients’ stays in the ED. By utilizing a combi-
nation of design elements, such as calling attention to those 
items requiring physician action and those not yet completed 
by other care providers, the work flow simplified the commu-
nication stages of an order’s progression. An example of a 
step-by-step work flow of the physician’s journey through 
obtaining a patient assignment, placing orders, and then 
discharging the patient can be seen in Figure 3.

We conducted the formative usability sessions during two 
development iterations, resulting in more than 30 usability 
findings. By performing several iterations of usability testing, 
our researchers refined and improved the quality of the indi-
vidual features that contributed to the overall work flow. The 
four features covered here in more detail demonstrate how 
design guidelines and user feedback produced changes during 
a series of design iterations.

Orders. A computerized physician order entry (CPOE) 
system enables providers to electronically specify medication 
orders as well as laboratory, admission, radiology, referral, 
and procedure orders. A tracking board is often used 
to display the statuses for these medications, laboratory 
orders, radiology orders, and electrocardiogram orders so 
physicians can easily see what is new or what is overdue. Our 
previous tracking board contained an abundance of icons 
with different meanings, and users demonstrated significant 
confusion in distinguishing their meanings and use. However, 
it became clear through observation that logical groupings 
and statuses were shared across these icons. Through use of 
the principles of data visualization and task prioritization, 
the icon sets associated with a category of orders could be 
combined into a single icon representing the order’s progress.
This new graphic, labeled as Initial Design in Figure 4, 
initially was an icon representing the order type and a set of 
indictors to signify the number of orders and the overdue 
threshold. However, results from the first round of usability 
testing determined that additional interaction was needed as 
well as an additional orderable type that occurs during some 

acute-care work flows: patient care orders. Iterations 2 and 3 
(Figure 4) show an actionable icon representing the impor-
tance of the available results and the time remaining in the 
current state (e.g., waiting on results). Additionally, during 
the first iteration of usability testing, physicians were not able 
to recognize levels of duration for events, described as a ring 
around the icon for the order type.
The final design, as seen at the bottom of the image in 
Figure 4, represents a set of orders for each category of medi-
cation, labs, imaging, and radiology, along with a progress 
indicator. The icon representation of the order(s) and the 
progress indicator use different degrees of color to indicate 
the stage of the order as well as the degree to which it is 
overdue.

Documentation work flow. Clinical documentation is the 
method by which physicians document patient care and 
communicate among providers across venues. Previous 
tracking boards in the form of free-text comments were often 
used to track provider comments regarding patient care plans. 
Therefore, our design goal was to allow the user to be able to 
complete documentation and view its status directly from the 
tracking board. By using consistent patterns to represent the 
status in the form of an icon that became the quick-access 
point, we achieved both goals.
Initial design concepts indicated different stages of 
documentation, including extra flags to identify multiple 
contributors, an extra indicator for academic hospitals to 
represent the need for an instructor signature, and different 
icons to represent the three stages of a document (e.g., “not 
started,” “in progress,” and “signed”). However, physicians 
found it difficult to recognize the different indicator levels for 
the documentation work flow beyond those of “not started,” 
“incomplete,” and “completed.” Therefore, the only steps in 
the documentation work flow that were needed were those 
representing the completion of the documentation and 
an additional indicator for academic or teaching hospitals 
requiring a cosign. 
The design progression, along with the final set of icons for 
displaying the status of the user’s documentation, is shown as 
the bottom set of icons in Figure 5. The icons for documenta-
tion work flow represent “not started” (blank note), “saved” 
or “incomplete” (note with text), “signed” or “completed” 
(note with green checkmark), and a “note requiring a cosign” 
(note with text and pencil icon).

Length-of-stay (LOS) meter. The LOS represents a patient’s 
current length of stay at each point in the ED work flow, from 
prearrival through the discharge process. LOS is shown as the 
number of minutes the patient has stayed in the ED, along 
with indicators for when the stay has exceeded a threshold 
(i.e., “overdue”) or has reached a second threshold (i.e., 
“critically overdue”). The LOS display is designed to alert the 
provider of the patient’s status as “unassigned to a provider,” 
“assigned to a provider,” or “time spent in the waiting room.”

The primary information pertaining to the length of time 
the patient has been in the ED work flow is shown face-up 
alongside the current point in the workflow. The initial LOS 
designs, shown in Figure 6 as the initial design and Iteration 
2 versions, were intended to illustrate the different points 
of the work flow as a single progress indicator. After initial 
observations, a single progress indicator was used for each 
point within the work flow, and the actual indication of the 
point in the ED visit was represented separately from the 
progress indicator. A third version of the design presented the 
threshold(s) for the current point in the ED visit within the 
progress indicator, as seen in Iteration 3.
Finally, usability testing demonstrated that physicians 
could successfully identify a patient’s point of treatment 
but had difficulty identifying the reference thresholds for an 
overdue or critically overdue LOS. Based on these findings, 
the final design added the current length of time in digital 
format and a progress indicator using color and contrast 
to indicate when the current length of time exceeded a 
threshold. This final design is shown as the bottom image in 
Figure 6.

Care delivery window. The care delivery window is a 
combined view of triage information, patient history, and 
order progress. It allows the user to quickly progress through 
each of the order types to see order statuses and their context 
within historical or current results. Therefore, the physician 
is presented with orderable information for each patient in a 
staged disclosure process. The progressive disclosure design 
pattern needed to help ED physicians prioritize information 

was initially designed using a series of hover boxes. As the 
user moved the mouse over the icon or design component, 
the hover boxes presented information related to each section 
of the screen. However, the physicians reported that the 
hover boxes were slower and more difficult to use when the 
physicians inadvertently invoked windows elements with an 
errant move of the mouse.
Usability feedback identified concerns with timing delays 
as well as navigation interactivity inherent in multiple hover 
boxes being invoked in rapid succession. Therefore, a modal 
panel was used to enable the user to confirm that something 
happened as a result of his or her click. The different inter-
active pieces were combined into a single window, which 
became an intentional action for something the user wants. 
This modal panel essentially behaves like a “one-window drill-
down” mini-application within the context of a single patient. 
The final design for the care delivery window that combines 
additional information for each component can be seen in 
Figure 7.

Delivering Results

During the development of ED LaunchPoint, it was impor-
tant for our team to maintain the paradigm of a tracking 
board while focusing on how the information supports user 
work flows. ED LaunchPoint achieved this goal through 
iterative changes and progression toward a solution that was 
most appropriate for the users and their needs. The updated 
appearance focuses on critical information and advanced 
viewing capabilities for complex information. By segmenting 
the tracking board into sections pertaining to patient-specific

Finally, after the overall solution was coded and available 
for release, usability researchers conducted validation studies 
to evaluate overall system usability. The findings from these 
summative studies are meant to validate and confirm the 
changes made during the formative evaluations. Few surprises 
should arise during this phase of testing given the iterative 
user‐centered design process used throughout the product 
development process. 
The final evaluations were performed remotely from the 
usability lab in Kansas City and included a total of 12 emer-
gency physicians using the most recent application code. 
Usability testing identified three new usability findings that 
were incorporated into the product’s development prior to 
its release. These findings fell into two categories of problems: 
visibility of system status and error feedback.
Design should be more than the product of a good idea 
or fixing existing issues. It should be the result of a process 
that opens up a multitude of possibilities generated through a 
vigorous design process. Our design changes had real impact,
