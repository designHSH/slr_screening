a b s t r a c t

This paper presents the InterMod methodology. By combining the widely accepted Agile
Methods, Model-Driven Developments and User-Centred Design it allows us to develop
high-quality interactive applications. As a main characteristic, it plans and organises the
software project as a series of iterations that are guided by the User Objectives in an
agile and user-centred manner. At each iteration, the software development work can be
distributed to different teams according to some developmental and integration activities.
Each activity is driven by models that are validated by a multidisciplinary team composed
of developers and users. The requirements are incrementally collected and formalised
by means of models based on user-centred design. Besides, the Semantically Enriched
Human–Computer Interaction model is proposed to speed up project validation. This model
enriches a human–computer interaction model with some visual characteristics and the
application semantic. Thus, the enriched model provides enough information to generate
prototypes so users and developers can easily validate this model. Diagram project is a real
case study that is used to illustrate the application of the InterMod methodology through
the whole paper.

1. Introduction

Agile Methods (AMs), Model-Driven Developments (MDDs) and User-Centred Design (UCD) are three approaches widely
accepted by the community that share a common objective of efficiency in the resulting software. However, none of them
on its own achieves success without encountering problems during application development. Therefore, efforts are being
made to integrate these techniques so that the advantages of one mitigate the problems of the others.

AMs and MDDs are the predominant approaches in Software Engineering. AMs are able to develop a software product
incrementally and iteratively. They get feedback from the client at each incremental delivery and, as a result, adapt the
development plan accordingly. Most studies report increased code quality when agile methods are used but also report a lack
of attention to design and architectural issues [1]. Moreover, Software Engineering principles directly derive the quality of
the software from good design. Current trends establish design as a set of final product models characterised by iterative and
incremental development while, at the same time, promote formal development along the lines of traditional or waterfall
methodologies. Some authors [2] point out a drawback of MDD: models are difficult to maintain because changes come up
and new requirements are added while a project is progressing.

On the other hand, UCD is the dominant approach in the area of Human–Computer Interaction. UCD involves the end
user in a multidisciplinary development process based on iterative design and evaluation, so the designer understands the
user’s needs and tasks [3,4]. But, in the same way as traditional or heavyweight methodologies, UCD needs all requirements
be gathered and evaluated before they can be implemented [5–7].

To make up for the weaker aspects of these trends, several proposals have emerged that integrate AMs into both MDD
[8,9] and UCD [10]. However, due to the fact that a majority of software engineering development processes focus on
software architecture, a satisfactory integration has not been achieved yet. Therefore, we have focused on integrating these
three techniques into one approach that takes into account the User Objective to guide developments.

The User Objective concept appears both in Usability Engineering and in classical software engineering for different
purposes. Usability Engineering uses both task analysis and scenarios as usual techniques to describe how users accomplish
tasks in an interface. Different authors in this area also mention usability goals [11,12], user’s motivations [6], user’s needs
[13] or user tasks [14,15]. On the other hand, traditional Software Engineering utilises Use Cases to describe the system
behaviour from the user point of view. Use Cases aim to express the functional requirements of the application in order to
configure the domain model. Cooper [6] states that: ‘‘Use cases permit a complete cataloguing of user tasks for different classes
of users but say little or nothing about how these tasks are presented to the user or how they should be prioritised in the interface’’.
Our User Objectives concept approximates or resembles to concepts developed by Usability Engineering (see Section 3.1).

In this paper we propose a new approach to software development that combines agile characteristics, model-driven
development and user-centred techniques. The main contributions of the paper can be summarised as follows:

c1. An agile methodology organised as a series of iterations based on User Objectives is proposed as a new way to promote a

correct development. This iterative approach guides the incremental development of software.
c2. The Semantically Enriched Human–Computer Interaction (SE-HCI) Model is proposed to support project requirements. SE-

HCI is a new model integrated in the Model Driven Process that allows usability and incremental evaluation. This model
is tested by a multidisciplinary team composed of developers and users, just as it is proposed by UCD.

This paper is structured as follows: Section 2 outlines the primary characteristics of agile methods and compares them
to the other above-mentioned approaches. Section 3 presents the InterMod methodology by means of a real case study. The
concept of User Objective, the activities and models of the proposed agile development are also described. The next section
explains the SE-HCI Model. Section 5 shows the InterMod process model and applies it to the case study. Finally, we draw
some conclusions and outline our future work.

2. Agile development process: virtues and defects

Agile software development establishes the following principles [16]: individuals and interactions over processes
and tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, and
responding to change over following a plan.

This approach challenges waterfall or heavyweight methods in which one activity begins only when the previous one
finishes and where extensive and well-founded documentation is required. The rationale behind these traditional methods
is to reduce the number of corrections further on in the process and consequently reduce the cost of the project. However,
this type of planning fails in practice, as it does not allow the changes that inevitably come up and are to be made during
development [17]. Because of this, versions of the agile philosophy such as eXtreme Programming [18], Scrum [19], Crystal
Clear [20], Feature Driven Development [21] or Unified Process [22] currently prevail among others.

2.1. Agile processes and user-centred design

One important aspect of UCD is the collaboration between users and developers to build software solutions, so that each
group brings its own experience [23]. According to Norman [24], first it is necessary to identify and model the needs of
future users, and later to involve those users in the iterative evaluations of the product. Thus, the purpose is to improve the
product’s usability in order to: reduce errors, satisfy users and facilitate its learning and use, as it is defined in ISO standard
9241-11 [25].

Both UCD and AM are centred on the user/client and apply an iterative development process with continuous testing.
These proposals contrast with traditional architecture-based development processes, which are directed by developers
whose first focus is to achieve a good implementation.

Nevertheless, UCD and AM show great differences related to how they act and what their interests are [10]. On the
one hand, the agile philosophy recommendation of flexibility in action to face changes is at odds with the UCD principle
of interface design prior to implementation (up-front). On the other hand, UCD develops a holistic product, while the agile
process is incremental and results in sub products. Besides, while agile methods focus on code development, UCD methods
focus on designing the interactions in which users will be engaged.

Finally, both approaches seek to satisfy the users’ needs but in different manners. AM involves users in checking the
correct implementation of the functionality; while UCD records users’ opinion about other aspects of the whole application,
such as satisfaction or use efficiency. UCD focuses on how end-users work with the system, whereas AM is more concerned
with how software should be built or how the process is managed.

2.2. Agile processes and model-driven development

MDDs use models mainly as documentation and guidance for the subsequent implementation phase. Although models
are fundamental for general engineering processes, they are underused in Software Engineering. Many developers consider
that modelling demands creating excessive and extensive documentation, which ultimately is of little help when it comes
time to implement and maintain the system [2]. The changes arising throughout development seem to cause difficulties in
updating the models. In fact, many developers skip the model re-design phases and prefer to modify the code directly.

From this point of view, two issues are in strong conflict in software development. On the one hand, as MDD needs
to maintain model consistency when changes come up during application development, the representing model must be
an accurate and updated abstraction of the system in progress [26]. On the other hand, due to unforeseen changes, AMs
perform modifications on the implementations that are not reflected in their designs. Therefore, if the designed model does
not correspond with the current implementation this model cannot be used to drive the future evolution of the project and
so this could spell its failure.

The Agile Modelling method defines a collection of principles and practices for effective modelling and for diminishing
documentation efforts. Agile Model Driven Development (AMDD) [27] proposes fast model-storming sessions, on a just-
in-time basis during development iterations, in order to analyse a requirement or to design a solution. Modelling sessions
usually involve two or three people, who discuss an issue while sketching on paper or a whiteboard. In the same way, other
agile methods propose several formalisms to model requirements: Use Cases are formalized on RUP or AUP projects; major
Features are identified on FDD [21] projects; and User Stories are written on XP projects.

3. InterMod, a new methodology proposal

InterMod is a methodology [28] whose aim is to help with the accurate development of interactive software, web design
included. Recent studies have led us to revisit it by integrating the three philosophies previously discussed, i.e. UCD, MDD
and AM, and by including User Objectives as guidelines for the developing process.

This proposal organises the project as a series of iterations, just as the agile methodologies do, and distributes the work
in iterations according to the different developmental and integration activities of the User Objectives. These processes can
be carried out in parallel by different teams. Each activity is driven by models and must be validated by a multidisciplinary
team composed of developers and end-users.

In the following subsections the different aspects of the methodology will be illustrated by means of a real case study: the
development of Diagram. The interactive application Diagram allows designers to easily build and validate user task models
constructed for interactive applications. So, once the theoretical aspects of the methodology are explained, they will be put
into practice on the example. The example part will be framed.

3.1. User objectives, the development guide

In this context, a User Objective (UO) is a user desire (e.g., ‘‘buying a t-shirt’’ or ‘‘reserving a meeting room in a workplace’’)
that can be achieved by one or more user functionalities. UOs are fundamentally determined with the collaboration of a
group of future users of the application. These UOs are gathered by means of Usability Engineering methods, and contrasted
with the quality criteria defined in usability standards [13]. UOs are gathered throughout the project using an agile process
and serve as a guide to developers (see Section 3.2).

The UO syntactical form includes a consecutive number ⟨i⟩, a name and brief description, i.e. UOi-name: description.

The first UO described in the Diagram project was:
UO0-Representing Task Diagram: Represent the decomposition of user tasks using HTA diagrams.

A UO can fit with one or more functional requirements (use cases) and/or non-functional requirements. Non-functional
requirements normally relate to efficiency, ergonomics or limitations due to user or device types. For example, in an
application for buying and selling apartments, the user not only wishes to ‘‘look for an apartment’’ but also ‘‘look quickly
for an apartment that I like, ask about the price and compare this price with the price of my own apartment’’ or ‘‘ask about the
price of an apartment and about credit by using my mobile phone’’. These UOs specialise various general cases: ‘‘look for an
apartment’’, ‘‘ask about price’’, ‘‘appraise an apartment’’, and ‘‘see credits’’.

UOs are formalised by describing the user’s behaviours (actions to complete) and the system behaviours (reaction of the
application), in terms of the interface. These descriptions include just a part of the functional requirements, as they only
indicate the system reactions on the interface. That is, they do not include the control reactions of the application such as
‘‘register purchase’’ or ‘‘save order line’’. UOs are useful to organise the interface and also to help to discover and to formalise
the use cases in later stages. Thus, they can be seen as abstractions of the use cases, while the use cases describe concrete
functional aspects.

Lansweerde [29], in his goal-oriented requirement-engineering proposal, uses the term ‘goal’ with a different meaning
of the ‘Objective’ in this proposal. So, goals are declarative statements of intents to be achieved by the system under
consideration. Meanwhile, the UOs here presented are defined by means of the operations that the end-user will perform on
the application interface.

3.2. Agile development process

InterMod process (Fig. 1) starts with step 0-Analyse the Overall Project at the beginning of the project, and then continues
with a sequence of iterations. Each iteration includes three steps: step 1.i-Build the User Objectives List, step 2.i-Plan the
Parallel Iteration and step 3.i-Perform Activities of the Iteration (i is the i-th iteration).

All iterations are guided by the same action plan that divides the work according to the Software Engineering activities
of different User Objectives (UOs). Every activity is driven by models (Fig. 1). And these last can be revised and modified as
a consequence of the integration processes. All the models can be revised and new UOs can be created even during the final
integration of software.
Step 0 — Analyse the Overall Project

At the beginning, the project as a whole must be analysed in order to determine: (a) the starting UOs, and (b) the
design decisions. The starting UOs (usually the most important or needed), together with a provisional but general menu
incorporating some functionalities, provide the global view of the application. All starting UOs will be direct UOs, i.e. UOs
proposed directly by the user. Also, it is important to draw up the models that help to collect the defining characteristics of
the system type (e.g., device type, security, window size, colour, logo, etc.) and those of the user (e.g., colour preferences,
font, size, some limitations as colour blindness, deafness, vision loss, etc.). These characteristics are collected in the System
Model and the User Model respectively. All these initial decisions will give coherence to the incremental process and all
developments in the project will inherit, supplement or extend these aspects.

The Diagram first UOs requested by users were:
UO0-Representing Task Model: Represent the decomposition of user tasks using HTA diagrams.
UO1- Simulating Tasks: Simulate the execution of user tasks in an automatically obtained prototype.
UO2- Printing Task Report: Print a report of the tasks that serves to document the Task Model.
UO3- Managing Project/UOs: Manage aspects of user task diagrams for a project/ UOs: new, open, save, and
save as.
Some aspects were taken into account in this step: (1) the software should be a desktop application; (2)
in order to evaluate user–system interactions, the prototypes should include a series of windows with
buttons; (3) the general appearance of the interface would consist of a display area for the task schema
and, at least, two initial menus —a ‘‘file menu’’ and an ‘‘edit menu’’; and (4) although different techniques
can be used to represent the task model, a simple hierarchical representation, such as HTA [39], and an
internal language based on XML were chosen.

Step 1.i — Build the User Objective List

The application requirements are incrementally collected during the progressive construction of the UOs List. Each
iteration updates this list with new UOs obtained either from new project needs (direct UOs) or from previous UOs (non-
direct UOs).

Fig. 2 shows the evolution of the UOs created in the successive iterations of the Diagram example.
Seven UOs were directly proposed by the user: {0, 1, 2, 3} in the iteration 0 (as described before), {8} in
the iteration 2 and {10, 11} in the iteration 3. For example the new direct UO at the iteration 2 was:
UO8-Exporting User Task Model: Export HTA diagram semantics to a XML language for describing user
interfaces (e.g. XIML, UIML or Teresa XML).

Non-direct UOs are derived UOs by division or fusion of previous UOs developments. A complex UO may be broken down
into two or more new UOs, and some simple or dependent UOs may be merged in one new integrated or Fusion UO.

For instance UO0 was divided into three UOs at iteration 1:
UO5-Visualizing User Task Model: Display the diagram as an HTA hierarchy in a section of the main window.
UO6-Manipulating/Creating User Tasks from buttons: Create, move and delete user tasks as direct buttons
functionalities.
UO7-Manipulating/Creating User Tasks from edit menu: Create, move and delete user tasks from the edit
menu.
Besides, UO2 and UO3 were merged into UO4 at iteration 1, and other fusions occurred at iterations 3, 4,
5, 6 and 8. The new Fusion UO4 was:
UO4- Managing File Menu: Managing aspects of user task model for UOs: new, open, save, save as and print
tasks.

In this way, the UOs List has been modified in the sense of agile methodologies [30]: after evaluations undertaken by
stakeholders, and considering criticism arising from meetings of members of the same and different teams.

Step 3.i –Perform Activities of the Iteration

Each team performs the activities established in the plan. All developmental and integration activities are driven by
models generated and evaluated during the project following the Object Management Group’s Model Driven Architecture
proposal [31].

Each developmental and integration activity gives rise to its corresponding model (Fig. 4). The activities DA-1.Analysis and
navigation design and IA-1.Requirements Models integration deal with the M-1.Requirements Model that includes the SE-HCI
Model (more detail in Section 4).

The DA-2.Interface Building creates the M-2.Presentation Model for a UO previously designed and evaluated, and the IA-
2.Interface Integration fuses together the M-2 of some UOs. The M-2 of a specific UO settles the graphical characteristics
gathered from the M-1. There are several languages for modelling user interfaces widely used and tested, such as XIML [32]
or UIML [33], and they may be used to reflect this model.

Finally, the DA-3.Business–Logic Coding and IA-3.Code Integration & Refactoring deal with the M-3.Functionality Model
that guides the implementation in a particular programming language. This model inherits the behaviour issues from the
UO M-1 evaluated in the first activity. UML or SysML [34] are alternative languages typically used to represent this model.

Feature-Driven Development (FDD) [21] is a close related methodology that uses MDD and divides the labour into
different features (e.g., ‘‘calculate the total of a sale’’ or ‘‘add a new customer to a customer list’’) to measure the project
progress. Use cases obtain the features that allow the domain objects to be modelled (class diagram and operations required
in the system). However, its main difference with InterMod is that it divides the labour on a user basis, that is by means of
UOs/user desires, instead of features that can be either user’s or system’s needs. Thus, InterMod models the tasks, navigation
and presentation of the user rather than the domain objects. Once the objectives have been evaluated in terms of usability

(c) ∀k {[k ∈(1..2) ∧UOi= divided_into({UOl, UOm, . . . }) ∧realized(DA-k(i)] →[(M-k(l), M-k(m),..) created & evaluated ∧

That is, the models (M-1, M-2) that have been completed for a UOi before its division into new UOs, implies that the same

For example, the activity DA-1(0) done in the iteration 0 created M-1(0) (proposition a). Besides, as UO0

and M-1(7) were considered already created as parts of M-1(0), as well as the DAs for these divided UOs.

∧(DA-k(l), DA-k(m), . . .) realized]}

Another interesting situation is related to a Fusion UOi and its fused UOs (UOl, UOm, . . .). The DA-k performed for a
Fusion UOi implies that the model M-k(i) has been created and evaluated (proposition a). In addition, it is also assumed that
the M-k of all fused UOs are already created and evaluated (as a part of this Fusion UOi model), and therefore the DA-k for
these UOs are considered already performed.

This case is illustrated with Fusion UO9- Simulating, Managing and Exporting task model. UO9 is the fusion
of {UO1-Simulating . . . , UO4-Managing . . . , UO8-Exporting . . .} (see iteration 3 in Fig. 2). The DA-2(9) (at
iteration 4 in Fig. 3) was performed and so M-2(9) was created and evaluated, as indicated in proposition

4. The SE-HCI model: supporting the project requirements

The requirements are incrementally collected and validated with models based on UCD. The designer’s team formalises
the requirements model (M-1) for each UO. As mentioned above, all developments in the project draw up the System and User
Models, especially at the beginning of the project. The requirements model includes the User Task and SE-HCI Models (see
Fig. 6). The User Task Model is a classic element in Model-Based User Interface Development [14,35,36] that describes user
performance in completing each UO.

The SE-HCI Model is the core of the proposed methodology. It gathers the requirements associated to the UO described
in the User, System and Task Models, and also incorporates:

(1) The system–user direct communication. The description of the actions that users and system may carry out at the user

interface level during an interactive session [14], and their possible temporal relationships are included here. Thus, it
generates those communications in which the system interacts directly with the user by displaying an error window or
a simple message. This means that the system’s operations on other elements in the application’s environment, e.g. a
database, will not be expressed in the model since they will not be involved in any direct communication with the user.
This direct communication will be taken into account to create Presentation and Functionality Models (M-2 and M-3).
(2) The basic characteristics gathered in the Prototype Model (colours, sections, button types, etc.) that draw the visual

aspects of the application. These will be taken into account to create the UO Presentation Model (M-2).
(3) The incorrect interactions of the application through interface navigation. Therefore, they define, together with the

correct ones obtained from the Behaviour Model of the User Task Model, the application semantic. Both types of
interactions express the different UO runs. This semantic will be taken into account to develop the corresponding UO
Functionality Model (M-3).

The Prototype and Behaviour Models provide the SE-HCI model with enough information to generate prototypes
automatically. Wiegers [37] points out the difficulty of visualising exactly how software will behave just by reading textual
requirements or by studying analysis models, and we agree: users are more willing to try a prototype than to read a
document. So, the generated prototypes allow and that designers, users and developers carry out the evaluation jointly
and easily.

Similarly to our proposal, Propp and colleagues [38] start with task models in the process of developing interactive
applications, then define the navigational structure, and finally create an Abstract User Interface (AUI) independent of the
device, and one or more Concrete User Interface(s) (CUI). During the development process they perform several usability
evaluations. There is no division of labour. The project is carried out in a block and the different iterations follow the steps
in the chain: Task Model-Dialogue Model-AUI-CUI.

Concerning the UserTask Model, the basis concept is the User Task, which allows capturing user performance. This concept
is complemented with the Order among tasks (Sequential, Indifferent, Choice), and with the task Type, which establishes
whether it is compulsory to carry out the task and the number of performances needed (Unitary, Optional, Repetitive).

determines the next task to carry out for each task by considering the task type and order.

6. Conclusions and future work

InterMod is a methodology whose aim is to help with the accurate development of high-quality interactive software. The
software project is planned and organised as a series of iterations that are guided by the User Objectives (UO) in an agile and
user-centred manner. A User Objective is a user desire that can be achieved by one or more user functionalities.

The proposal here presented integrates three philosophies: AM, UCD and MDD. From the point of view of Agile Methods,
the work is organised in a series of iterations in which the User Objectives to be dealt with are developed. In the first analysis,
the initial User Objectives are obtained and then distributed among the workgroups according to different activities. Each
iteration is open to include new User Objectives, whether obtained through previous refinements or through evolution or
alterations during the agile development of the application itself.

For developing the UOs, InterMod proposes developmental and integration activities. The possibility to distribute
the work in parallel increases the speed of resolution, although the process itself requires integration points to ensure
consistency. This methodology allows the gathering and validation of the requirements incrementally. Because of this
agile approach, InterMod, unlike UCD, does not require the complete development of the application interface before the
implementation of the business logic, but assures usability. In order to ensure the coherence of the resulting application,
integration activities and validations from the perspective of requirements, interface and software models have been
included.

InterMod follows the guidelines of MDD but improves its lack of consistency by means of the continuous integration of
models in the agile process, which update all generated models. The SE-HCI model allows the creation of prototypes that
facilitate the joint participation of end-users, designers and developers in the evaluation processes, as recommended by
UCD and AM. The most frequent criticism to agile methodologies is their lack of formality. Obtaining a product is prioritized
over the time invested in its design. Including different models, such as SE_HCI in navigation analysis and design activities,
requires the formalization of the design prior to its implementation.

The demonstrator Diagram has been developed according to the InterMod methodology. The iterative process started
with a small group of initial UOs, which evolved and, incorporating new UOs, produced new Diagram versions. This paper has
shown one of these initial versions. At present, Diagram continues as an active project. Comparing to classical methodologies,
our agile user-centred methodology has proved: (1) its flexibility on planning and facing unexpected changes that appear
through the project life by means of ‘‘user desires’’, and (2) the early validation of the models included in SE-HCI allows to
progress and guide subsequent activities of the UOs.
