Human-centered Design Approach toward the 
Physical Activity Initiative for Work-from-Home 

Workers during the COVID-19 Outbreak 

 
Juhri Selamet,1 Universitas Multimedia Nusantara, Indonesia 
 
Abstract: Physical activity levels have been declining since the COVID-19 outbreak due to social distancing and 
working from home. With respect to the modifiable risk factor of work-from-home (WFH) workers’ physical inactivity, 
the present study seeks to develop a physical activity initiative for them during this global pandemic. Using the human-
centered design (HCD) approach with the online involvement of twenty-two WFH workers, the results of this study 
presented the assessment and insights from the sequential HCD process regarding WFH workers’ workstation in the 
home environment, views on received and tracked daily physical activities during the outbreak, and feedback from a 
five-day live physical activity prototyping. This study discusses the systematic development of the HCD approach in 
designing a physical activity initiative. However, a low number of participants may limit the conclusions drawn from 
this research. In conclusion, the HCD approach provided an opportunity to engage WFH workers and create and 
design with them to promote physical activity during a pandemic. After being developed through design development, 
being examined through hands-on use, and tested on the target group in pandemic settings, this present study informs 
and provides insights to design practitioners to consider the practical design of health workers during a pandemic.  

 
Keywords: Covid-19 Outbreak, Health Initiative, Human-centered Design (HCD), Physical Activity,  

Work-From-Home (WFH) 

 

Introduction 

On January 30, 2020, the COVID-19 outbreak was declared by World Health Organization 
(WHO) as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (WHO 2020a; Mahtani et al. 
2020) and then proclaimed as a pandemic on March 11, 2020 (WHO 2020b). Globally, this 
outbreak has impacted people’s activities, health, and well-being (UNDP 2020; Fore 2020). The 
coronavirus mainly spreads through close contact (Rothan and Byrareddy 2020; Sohrabi et al. 
2020) via small droplets induced by sneezing, coughing, and talking (WHO 2020c; CDC 2020). 
People have been advised to practice social distancing (Wilder-Smith and Freedman 2020), 
leading government officials in many countries to regulate their citizens by ensuring they stay at 
home and practice physical distancing to fight the virus (Viner et al. 2020). Subsequently, 
similar to many countries, in Indonesia also, under government regulations, companies or 
institutions have been instructed to operate from home (DJalante et al. 2020; Ryalino 2020). 
Public- and private-company workers in Indonesia have started working remotely from their 
home, and thus, they are now called work-from-home (WFH) workers.. 

Although working from home reduces the possibility of being afflicted with the virus, this 
circumstance poses a new health problem. Unlike in developed countries, where companies 
help employees to set up a workstation at home (Harvard Business Review 2020; Nova 2020; 
Morel 2020), WFH workers in Indonesia receive little support from their employers. Some have 
a workstation with limited facilities while some do not even have a table (Umah 2020). The 
unfriendly working environment at home and limited access to the outdoor environment to

Physical (In)Activity 

Phyical activity is defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles, resulting in 
energy expenditure. In daily life, physical activity can be categorized into occupational, sports, 
conditioning, household, or other activities requiring a minimum of 150 minutes per week 
(Caspersen, Powell, and Christenson 1985; WHO 2010a). In contrast, physical inactivity is a 
term used to identify people who do not perform the WHO recommended level of regular 
physical activity (WHO 2010a, 53; The US New York Health n.d.). 

While physical activity is associated with numerous health benefits, for both physical and 
mental health (Janssen and LeBlanc 2010), physical inactivity is a significant contributor to 
mortality. According to the WHO (Warburton et al. 2006; WHO 2010b), approximately 3.2 
million deaths worldwide annually can be attributed to physical inactivity. It is a modifiable risk 
factor for cardiovascular disease and a widening variety of other chronic diseases, including 
diabetes mellitus, cancer (colon and breast), obesity, hypertension, bone and joint diseases 
(osteoporosis and osteoarthritis), and depression (WHO 2010a; WHO 2010b). Apparently, since 
the COVID-19 outbreak, physical activity levels have been declining (Hall et al. 2020; 
Jakobsson et al. 2020; Hammami et al. 2020) due to social distancing and working from home. 

The Human-centered Design (HCD) in Health Research 

Human-centered design (HCD) is an approach to developing a design solution by involving the 
human or potential users’ perspectives and learning from them in all steps of the design process 
(Steen 2011). HCD “is based on the use of techniques that communicate, interact, empathize 
and stimulate the people involved, obtaining an understanding of their needs, desires, and 
experiences which often transcends that which the people themselves actually realized” 
(Giacomin 2014, 610). Scholars have examined the HCD in a variety of contexts and settings, 
specifically in the context of health. Previous research on the HCD has been used to develop an 
online intervention for the self-management of asthma (Morrison et al. 2015) and concepts to 
stimulate a vibrant local economy in the Oakland Best Babies Zone for infant mortality and 
community health (Vechakul, Shrimali, and Sandhu 2015). The HCD was explicitly used to 
understand the situation by collecting and analyzing data, developing a new clinical decision 
support system, and implementing and evaluating the system across twenty-four clinics in the 
study of Catalani et al. (2014). Furthermore, the HCD was used to design a backpack, which 
decreased the load on the shoulders, neck, and waist (Amiri, Dezfooli, and Mortezaei 2012), 
and global health education programs (Adam et al. 2019). Although the HCD has proved to be 
an effective approach to engage with stakeholders, users, and patients (Ragouzeos et al. 2018; 
Aifah et al. 2020), there is still a lack of research on HCD in the physical activity area. Thus, 
there exists an exciting opportunity to explore the HCD approach to promote physical activity 
among WFH workers during a pandemic. 

Study Objective 

This study aimed to develop a physical activity design initiative for WFH workers using the 
principles of the HCD in a pandemic setting. The initiative aims to incorporate better physical 
activity health literacy and support tracking activities into the daily physical activity routine of 
WFH workers. Through a sequential HCD process, this study described a comprehensive 
approach to use the HCD to generate physical activity design outcomes and initiative to inform 

 
 

design and health professional practitioners, as well as contribute to the HCD research in 
pandemic settings. 

Methods and Materials 

A version modified by previous researchers of the IDEO approach to the HCD was applied in 
this study (IDEO 2015; Aifah et al. 2020). By using the principles of the HCD approach, this 
study aimed to develop a physical activity design initiative for WFH workers to stay active at 
home during a global pandemic. The core tenets of the HCD included in this study follow three 
sequential phases: inspiration, ideation, and implementation. Each phase involved key activities 
(Ragouzeos et al. 2018; Aifah et al. 2020). Empathizing and defining activities were included in 
the inspiration phase via interviews. Ideation covered design conceptualizing and rapid 
prototyping activities via co-creation. Implementation was incorporated with live prototyping 
and testing evaluation activities. The present study was conducted during the COVID-19 
outbreak. Therefore, to abide by government order and regulation to practice physical/social 
distancing, data collection and all other activities were performed online.  

Participants 

Participants were selected through convenience sampling. They were sent emails, inquiring 
about their willingness to participate in this research study. Information about their 
characteristics, including information about their age and sex, was obtained. Participants were 
recruited during the first stage of study, and consent to participate in the research (brief 
demographic survey, online interviews, and workshop) was obtained through a written form. 
Participants were compensated for their participation. Participants provided consent through a 
written form prior to participation. 

The HCD Process and Physical Activity Design Initiative Development 

Phase I—Inspiration: Interview of WFH Workers 

The inspiration phase involved empathizing and defining activities via online interviews (Zoom 
video meeting). Observation of participants’ work environment was also conducted through the 
live video meeting. This phase aimed to empathize with and understand and define the 
challenges WFH workers experience in performing physical activity during a global pandemic. 
To do so, participants were asked to take a picture of their home workstation and show it. 
Furthermore, participants were asked about their physical activities, sitting time, and how they 
manage their physical activities daily during the outbreak, especially given the limitations to 
stepping outside the house. They were also questioned about their knowledge and 
recommendations about and the challenges they experience related to physical activity during 
the outbreak. The interview lasted approximately fifteen minutes and consisted of five main 
questions. Twelve WFH workers participated in the interview.  

Phase II—Ideation: Co-creation with WFH Workers 

For phase 2, participants were asked to attend an online co-creation workshop via a Zoom 
meeting. Co-creation is an opportunity to “convene a group of people you are designing for and 
then bring them into the design process” (IDEO 2015). Therefore, two main activities were 
performed as part of this phase: design conceptualization and ideation with rapid prototyping. 
While design conceptualization aims to enable participants to extend the practice of empathy to 
visioning, i.e., developing concrete solutions or ideas for design outcome (Ragouzeos et al. 
2018), ideating with rapid paper prototyping is an effective way to make ideas and concepts 
tangible. Besides, rapid paper prototyping is an efficient design technique to learn and discuss 
through making for obtaining critical feedback from participants quickly. Co-creation was run 

through a forty-minute online workshop to discuss physical activity health literacy resources, 
self-tracking, visual design, and thoughts on rapid paper prototyping. Eight WFH workers 
joined the ideation phase. All of them had participated in the previous phase (inspiration).  

Phase III—Implementation: Live Prototyping and Testing  

Phase III incorporated information from phases I and II into the iteration design of prototype 
design outcomes. Seventeen participants participated in a five-day prototype testing. Seven 
were involved in phases I and II. New participants (N = 10) were asked to partake in the live 
prototype testing. All participants were informed they would receive a series of mailings on 
physical health information during a five-day timeframe. They were asked to self-report and 
track their daily physical activity via journaling. On the final day, participants were asked to fill 
out a self-reported online questionnaire to assess and share their experience on the live 
prototype testing.  

Data Collection and Analysis 

Recordings, notes, Zoom screen captures, digital photographs, and prototypes generated from 
the interviews and focus groups were analyzed to establish a series of themes, insights, and 
reflections at the end of each phase based on preparation activities and questions in previous 
studies related to the HCD approach (Ragouzeos et al. 2018; Aifah et al. 2020). Additionally, 
this study included descriptive statistics used to describe the quantitative results of the 
implementation phase (Pocuca et al. 2015; Selamet and Simpson 2019). Bootstrapping was 
deployed to account for the small sample size of live prototyping (N = 17) and any potential 
skew. This synthesized data was used to establish goals related to the types of support, 
participant understanding, and experiences stemming from designing physical activity 
initiatives.

Global Pandemic and Condition of WFH Workers 

In general, participants acknowledged the government’s stay-at-home orders, limiting outside 
activities. Moreover, the order impacted their employers, who instructed workers to work from 
home. However, participants, in general, were not well prepared to follow this command. 
Moreover, their employers did not provide any support to set up workplace stations at home. 
Participant IN-K said, “I live in four-by-four meters square room; I do not have a worktable. I 
work on my bed sadly.” Participant IN-U stated, “I used to walk around my office during the 
break or take a walk outside the office, like a 15-minute walk; [I] cannot do that now. I sit more 
since working at home, and I am getting fat.” Participant IN-H also stated the same: “this 
sudden adjustment and transformation that I have not experienced in my ten years of career. At 
the office, I used to take stairs or go to my friend’s desk to talk; now, with this online meeting, 
it all sits, sit and sit; I did not have experience working at home. With this condition, I can tell I 
sit more than before.” Participants voluntarily sent pictures of their workstation at home to 
illustrate their working conditions (see Figure 1).

Health Resources on Physical Activity during a Pandemic 

All participants received emails from their employers on basic health practices one should 
adhere to during a pandemic, such as washing hands and wearing a mask. Some even received a 
tracking app from their company to track their health so they could directly report to the 
company whether they have coronavirus. However, none of them received any information 
regarding physical activity health resources while performing WFH activities. Participants felt 
those resources would have helped advocate and remind them to stay active and healthy. 
Participant IN-J said, “it may seem small details, but the employee needs a health literacy 
resources; small tips for WFH workers would help.” IN-L expressed the same desire: 
“physically and mentally, I am not all prepared for this WFH situation, so small advocacy what 
to do during this WFH moment would be great.” Participant IN-E stated, “[I] received nothing 
from my employment about how to stay active during this pandemic. So, I look up online 
literacy. Then I overwhelmed with all the information. I concern about the credibility of some 

 
 

information that I have found online. It would be great if I could receive health literacy from a 
well-known institution. I need credible health information.” 

Motivation for Physical Activity 

In general, participants needed the motivation to stay active at home. They were concerned 
about the impact of physical inactivity on their health and well-being. Typically, participants 
acknowledged their limitations such as space but were willing to overcome the situation and 
work on their health. “A reminder with some health info, I think to me, may encourage me to 
stay active at home,” stated participant IN-A. Participant IN-H vocalized, “regular daily health 
literacy information and tracking activity that I can control or self-management that would help 
me to stay active.” Participant IN-G said, “I honestly lost track sometimes, like times and days, 
you know when you stay at home alone for too long. So, I don’t know if I am being active or 
not.” Participants thought tracking physical activity would help motivate them to stay active, 
while simultaneously arguing they needed practical information to guide them to do a physical 
activity while working from home.

Need for Frameworks for Physical Activity Information 

While many participants recognized that information on physical activity resources could be 
easily found online, they, however, intended to acquire appropriate information on physical 
activity content from credible sources targeted to them. In general, participants felt comfortable 
following physical activity guidelines from highly credible resources. Participant ID-A 
suggested “a clear framework guidelines would benefit my physical activity behavior, and 
please make sure that physical activity guidelines not for a targeted athlete so a person like me 
can do it.” Therefore, to facilitate this need, WHO frameworks and guidelines were presented.  

On Designing and Delivering Physical Activity Communication Materials 

In general, participants rejected all text content with no visuals and recommended using more 
visuals. During the online co-session, participant ID-R said, “visual information helps me to 
stay engaged.” Participants discussed how visual messages should be clear, simple, and easy to 
read. Moreover, they expressed the fear of not being able to engage with the extended 
information and suggested having information up to five minutes of reading. Paper-based 
prototypes were presented for discussion to iterate and facilitate the co-creation meeting. 

All co-creation participants said they had prepared to receive health literacy resources on 
physical activity through their work email. Participant ID-K stated, “I am sitting more during 
this [working-from-home] time, especially in the weekdays; a piece of physical activity 
information deliver through my work email will remind me to, I do not know, stand up and 
walk around my room, maybe.” Agreeing with ID-K, participant ID-H said, “yes, work email 
would be great to deliver this information. I check my work email more often during this 
pandemic, maybe because I mostly spend my day at home looking to my computer screen.” 

On Tracking Physical Activity 

Co-creation started with a discussion on the framework and design of the physical activity and 
its information content, respectively. The final part of the discussion pertained to tracking 
physical activity. While participants recognized that literacy information on physical activity 
via email might encourage and remind them to perform physical activity, they wanted to put 
their knowledge from the content into action to track and manage it. Tracking physical activity 
emerged from the discussion. Although various participants wanted to have a digital app, they 
did not want to spend more money on purchasing it. Further, participants outlined the need for 
physical activity tracking that could be easily personalized. A physical-activity-tracking journal 
was presented. From the series of sketch paper prototypes, participants suggested being 
provided with the option to write in their journal online or offline. In other words, they wanted 
to have the option to both write and type to track their physical activity. While participant ID-K 
wanted a feature that could summarize the duration of the physical activity, participant ID-R 
added, “I want my journal to have space for writing notes. I like notes.”

Receiving Health Literacy Information on Physical Activity  

Overall, Table 4 shows that the participants liked receiving health literacy information on 
physical activity during the COVID-19 outbreak. Participants scored the following variables a

Discussion 

The prototype of the physical activity design initiative for WFH workers was developed using 
an iterative HCD process. This initiative aimed to support and motivate WFH workers during 
the COVID-19 outbreak. During the development process, this study carefully followed the 
HCD approach to assess and consider WFH workers’ views and participation through 
inspiration, ideation, and implementation. Therefore, this study led to an initiative adaptation 
that was WFH-worker-driven (participants), with testing evaluation presented.  

Results from the HCD process informed the uniqueness of this physical activity design 
initiative in several ways. First, the inspiration phase/stage provided the opportunity to learn and 
understand WFH workers’ current work conditions during a global pandemic. It informed and 
recognized their condition, and their interests, motivation, and views on how they can be best 
supported in their physical health activity were identified. Specifically, it was only through this 
process that WFH workers’ work environment at home could be observed online and learned. 
Results and insights from this stage became the preliminary recommendation for the ideation 
phase. 

Second, the literature suggested that the HCD promises to facilitate deep and meaningful 
involvement of participants in developing the design and testing the prototype (Giacomin 2014; 
Ragouzeos et al. 2018; Aifah et al. 2020; Tjandra, Pinasthika, and Winantyo 2020). Therefore, 
informed by doing so in Phase I, Phase II (ideation) was conducted to accommodate the 
involvement of participants in design conceptualization and development through co-creation. 
The ideation phase provided a critical aspect to understand that information on physical activity 
needs to uncover clear physical activity guidelines that WFH workers can follow. Additionally, 
this study discovered the participants’ desire to put their knowledge into action through this 
process. While conceptualizing physical information literacy content and delivery scenario, 
suggestions to put health literacy into action were developed into tracking physical activity. 
Insights from paper-based prototype testing design were useful in discussing design 
development. Without ideation, design direction might have easily gone unnoticed, and 
participants would have been unlikely to access and use the design outcome, had they found it 
interesting, and reflect on and represent their current condition. 

Third, by performing the implementation phase, this study presents live prototyping and 
testing evaluation. Phases I and II were informed about prototype design development before it 
went to testing. Evaluation of live prototype testing revealed that a critically perceived benefit 
of the initiative was the importance of physical health literacy and physical activity tracking to 
support WFH workers’ needs during a pandemic. Findings of the live prototype testing yield 
valuable feedback regarding WFH workers’ communication design and tracking system 
preferences. Together, results from the live prototype testing highlight that, in general, WFH 
workers showed keen interest in and support for the initiative aimed to help them stay active 
during the outbreak.  

Finally, this study yields the HCD process in approaching participatory engagement from 
the beginning, mainly as it ensures outcomes are driven by unique preferences, feedback, and 
circumstances of the potential target group. Similar to previous studies (Ceschin and Gaziulusoy 
2016; Ragouzeos et al. 2018; Aifah et al. 2020), it shows how the HCD offers the opportunity to 
define, create, and develop WFH- worker-driven solutions based on their current condition. 
Through the HCD, this study presents an effort to test and evaluate live prototyping to learn 
how this physical activity initiative is perceived by WFH workers. 

Limitations 

This study had limitations. The number of participants in the inspiration (N = 10), ideation (N = 
8), and live prototype testing evaluation phases (N = 17) were small, raising the question 
whether a larger sample group of WFH workers could have led to more feedback, insights, and 
opportunities for rigorous design outcomes. There is a debate among the HCD researchers 
regarding the most cost-efficient number of participants in an HCD process (Selamet and 
Simpson 2019). Vilardaga et al. (2018) argued that the most traditional approach suggested a 
sample size of five. However, recruiting WFH workers during the outbreak is particularly 
challenging, and thus, the author believes this study’s sample size was justified. 

The HCD process could have been applied more rigorously by including all key 
stakeholders in the study. As reported by previous studies (Aifah et al. 2020), participation from 
key stakeholders of different backgrounds and expertise may be impacted by intervention, 
initiative, or program. Future studies should put effort into including all key stakeholders in an 
HCD process. Additionally, a short five-day period of live prototyping without a control group 
may limit the conclusions drawn from the present study. Future work that fully implements the 
HCD process should include an evaluation of the design initiative’s health outcomes, as 
suggested by Bazzona et al. (2017). 

Conclusion 

In summary, principles of the HCD approach were systematically used to develop a prototype 
of a physical activity initiative that supports WFH workers to stay active at home during the 
challenging times of a global pandemic with limitations on going outside. The use of this 
iterative HCD approach provided an opportunity to engage WFH workers and create and design 
with them to understand their current work environment and situation. More importantly, the 
current study presented live testing and evaluation of the prototype and generated feedback, 
which can be used to enhance the literature for future studies related to the HCD.  

Moreover, after being developed through design development, being examined through 
hands-on use, and being tested on the target group in a pandemic setting, this present study may 
inform and provide insights to design practitioners working on the effective design about the 
health of workers during a pandemic.
