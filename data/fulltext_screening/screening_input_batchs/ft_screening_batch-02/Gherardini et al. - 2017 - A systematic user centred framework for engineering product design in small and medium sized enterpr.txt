A systematic user-centred framework for engineering product
design in small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)

Francesco Gherardini1
& Cristina Renzi2 & Francesco Leali1

Received: 22 June 2016 /Accepted: 1 December 2016 /Published online: 20 December 2016
# Springer-Verlag London 2016

Abstract The systematic integration of user needs in the
product design is a key issue in industry, especially for
small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), which suffer a
lack of engineering methods and resources. Moreover, most of
the approaches described in the literature are not flexible
enough to be tailored on the SMEs’ needs, involve users only
in the early design phases and are not fully accessible due to
the high cost in their implementation. The present paper pro-
poses a user-centred design methodological framework spe-
cifically focused on SMEs, which supports the designer from
both design and manufacturing aspects along the engineering
product design process. The framework integrates engineering
methods with Web-based software tools, which enable the
communication and the concurrent work of the design team,
and supports direct participation of users. Beside state-of-the-
art methods, the framework allows the integration of specifi-
cally tailored techniques. The framework is successfully val-
idated through an industrial case study developed in collabo-
ration with an Italian SME. As a result, the design of an
injection-moulded housing and the related interfaces of a bio-
medical electronic device are achieved with a reduction of
uncertainty and development time, by involving users
throughout the design phases and suggesting methods and

tools on the basis of the designers’ know-how and SME’s
specific resources.

Keywords User-centred design . Product design . SMEs .
Design methodology . QFD . Decision theory

1 Introduction

The importance of user understanding and user involvement
throughout the design process is a key issue in the develop-
ment of new successful products [1, 2]. However, in the tra-
ditional design process, while the designer operates over all
the phases of the design process, the user is usually involved
only in the initial stages, for product requirement identifica-
tion purposes. His tasks are related to engineering require-
ments and marketing requirements [3]. The former essentially
involve design, manufacturing and technical fields, other than
corporate, regulatory and physics. The latter directly derive
from the user needs and involve multi-disciplinary fields, such
as market research, economics, cognitive science and social
science.
In the planning phase, the designer is required to handle
uncertainties in data and information, individuate problem
causes and roots [4] and include the user needs into product
specifications. In the conceptual design phase, the designer
collects and integrates the preferences of the decision makers,
in order to generate design alternatives and to select the most
suitable ones. In the embodiment design phase, a compromise
product design is achieved, as well as the complete architec-
ture of the assembly. In the detail design, the designer has to
define part geometry, select the components materials, assign
tolerances, define the industrial equipment and optimize
manufacturing cost [5]. Since the development of competitive
products requires a strong interaction between designer and

user [6], the search for systematic integration of user needs in
the design process involves companies of all sizes, even if
small- and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) appear to be
the most affected due to their specific nature. Some literature
remarks related to SMEs and their needs in the product devel-
opment phases are provided in the following.
As stated by the European Commission [7], SMEs are
companies that employ less than 250 employees and of which
the annual turnover is less than 50 million euros or of which
the total assets are less than 43 million euros. SMEs are the
backbone of the economy in most countries. In the 28 coun-
tries of the European Community (EU28), there were 21.2
million SMEs in the “non-financial business” sector (i.e. in-
dustrial sector) in 2013. SMEs account for 99.8% of all enter-
prises in this sector, 66.8% of total employment and 57.9% of
total value added generated by the non-financial business sec-
tor [8]. Even more, in some countries such as Italy, the SMEs
account for 79.6% of total employment and 69.5% of total
value added generated by the industrial sector [9].
The SMEs’ investments in the skill and innovation field are
poor, since technological innovation tends to be the domain of
larger enterprises [8]. However, this business innovation ca-
pacity does not translate into outward innovation, either
through collaborating with other businesses (innovative
SMEs collaborating with others 11.69%; Italy 4.41%) or sell-
ing innovative products.
According to these statistics, most authors argue that SMEs
present specific needs in the product design due to their own
nature. Consequently, this individuality of needs originates a
multitude of self-made design approaches in SMEs that, ac-
cording to many authors, are generally unstructured and lack
of systematic engineering methods. These specific issues are
mainly a consequence of multi-disciplinary factors involved
in SME structure, as proposed in the following list:

–
Relationship between decisional process and inner hier-
archy: many SMEs share the overlap between managers,
leaders and owners. In [10], Nicholas et al. underline the
shorter decision-making process in SMEs due to fewer
layers of management, their greater functional integration
and less resistance to change. The main disadvantage in
new product development (NPD) that the SMEs’ practi-
tioners have to overcome is that SMEs are generally dom-
inated by the owner’s or the chief executive’s personality.
Accordingly, in [11], March-Chorda et al. state that in the
NPD process, contrary to what the theoretical studies rec-
ommend, the most frequent sequence of activities is rath-
er simple and short: “original idea–brief development–
prototype–fabrication”, with obvious lack in the process.
It mainly depends on the managers, who display a clear
tendency to overvalue their contribution in the process of
product innovation and centralize (and generally shorten)
the decisional phases. Decker et al. [12] underline that

SMEs do not change their managers as often as big en-
terprises, aiming to concentrate on long-term strategies
or—at the same time—precluding different changes in
their strategies.
–
Business culture based on subjectivism: As a direct con-
sequence of the previous point, SMEs differ from large-
sized enterprises in the successful application of engineer-
ing methods. In [13], a survey on 139 SMEs and 64 large
organizations reveals that SMEs mainly use subjective
approaches to prioritize their projects, mainly based on
personal beliefs, feelings, experiences and common
sense, instead of approach based on proven methodolo-
gies and real facts. De Toni and Nassimbeni [14] under-
line that the NPD process is not adequately studied in
SME and there is a lack of models and tools specifically
focused for these units. When compared with large-sized
enterprises, the innovative process in SMEs lacks of a
formal structure, so systemic innovation is not adequately
supported.
–
Lack of knowledge and innovation management:
Systematic knowledge management and, in particular,
the interaction with the final user, aiming at identifying
product requirements, are still prerogatives of large-sized
enterprises [15], which are particularly conscious of the
role of the user in the NPD and of the collaborative
chance represented by Web and social media spaces.
Differences in knowledge management (in particular in
knowledge elicitation) between SMEs and large-sized en-
terprises have been addressed also in [16]: SMEs gener-
ally do not use knowledge elicitation techniques but sim-
ply ask expert to jot down what they know. Moreover,
there is a lack of systematic knowledge sharing that is
based on unstructured phases of collaboration between
experts, or between experts and novices, or on the use
of professional forums on the Internet. Urban and
Hauser [17] underline how large-sized companies, as in
the automotive industry, explore a set of methodologies
to use Web and social media information to identify new
product opportunities by using dedicate applications and
Web-based advisers. Corporations need for strategies to
address the threats posed by Web, and they must decide
how to participate, even by changing their business
models to better fit the new online environment [18].
These new behaviours and new strategies are faced by
large-sized companies by developing dedicated Web en-
vironment for collecting user needs and preferences such
as in [19].
–
Inhomogeneous levels of human resource organization
and time: Kamsties et al. [20] list, among the SMEs’
specific problems, their lack of strategic issues in quality
and process improvements mainly due to daily tasks that
overwhelmed employees and management, the lack of
cooperation with external consultants and the lack of

know-how transfer on high-level issues. In [14], the au-
thors underline that the concurrent approach in the NPD
is not favoured and the interaction with other companies
is limited, mainly due to the strong division of labour that
characterizes SMEs. Similarly, in [21], Beylier et al. un-
derline differences in the organizational model of human
resources focusing on SMEs whose product design pro-
cess is mainly based on engineering simulation and com-
putational mechanics. The main trend is to outsource
these engineering design activities, either by developing
centralized specialized in-house services or by
subcontracting it to external firms providing engineering
analysis services. In both cases, the teams of professionals
of these SMEs do not operate as project teams, but each
engineer is in charge of one or more projects and works
independently by simulating and analysing the behaviour
of mechanical devices by means of computer-aided engi-
neering (CAE) software.
–
Presence of barriers to innovation due to resource avail-
ability: Cordeiro and Vieira [22] underline the presence of
internal or external barriers to innovation due to the dif-
ficulty in recruiting adequate human resources, the limi-
tation in the allocation of financial resources, the lack of
market information and technology and the lack of
funding and incentives, noting that the barriers observed
in Portugal match with studies carried out in other coun-
tries. In [23], the lack of resources and economies of scale
is presented as the main reason in methodological differ-
ences between SMEs and large-sized enterprises in the
successful application of new engineering methods.
Focusing on information technology (IT), Blackwell
et al. [24] deal with the SMEs’ absence of confidence to
make IT-related decisions, mainly due to the lack of per-
sonnel, IT expertise: SMEs are in difficulty when
investing time and capital into an integrated systems pro-
ject of which they might have little or no understanding.
Moreover, the authors note the higher need for informa-
tion integration in SMEs than in large-sized enterprises,
mainly due to the SMEs’ fewer financial resources and
smaller budgets than larger companies. In [25], a compar-
ison between SMEs and large-sized firms is based on the
adoption of ITand IT management capabilities. SMEs are
slow to adopt IT and information systems (IS), including
a shortage of slack resources for IT investment. However,
Beylier et al. [21] underline the need for IT tools to en-
hance collaboration, starting with an investigation carried
out within engineering analysis offices of SMEs.

In this complex and interdisciplinary scenario, the adoption
of accessible systematic approach for involving users during
all the design phases may represent a support for a better
integration of SMEs in the industrial competition and, there-
fore, is an open topic in the engineering research community.

The main question that this work tries to answer is “is it
possible to define an approach able to support SMEs’ practi-
tioners with focusing on proposing, and not imposing, tools
and methods, in order to understand and involve users
throughout the design process?”
In particular, the aim of proposed framework is to achieve
the following research goals:

–
Support the designers systematically, on the basis of
structured and objective methods, and help them by sug-
gesting actions (“what to do”), methods and tools instead
of subjectivism and personal beliefs, in particular, in the
decisional phases;
–
Define the role of the users in the design process, sug-
gesting “when” and “how” the designers need to interact
with them, in order to provide new strategic issues in the
design process improvement, from the input up to the
assessment phases;
–
Suggest methods and tools that are aligned with the
SME’s know-how but, contemporary, that can represent
an important training opportunity in order to enlarge the
designers’ knowledge;
–
Integrate the voice of customers and user needs in the
design process, by supporting their translation into prod-
uct requirements through objective methods and by the
recurrent involvement of the users in the whole design
process, in order to base the decisional and selection
phases on substantial information.

The paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 proposes a liter-
ature review, focusing on user-centred design approaches ap-
plicable to SMEs. Section 3 describes the proposed user-
centred design framework and its phases and presents the
need-based functional deployment, which we develop as a
revised quality function deployment (QFD) method.
Section 4 shows the application of the proposed framework
to an SME design problem, involving the design of an indus-
trial biomedical product. Finally, in Sect. 5, strengths and lim-
itations of the frameworks are discussed, and Sect. 6 reports
some concluding remarks.

2 Literature review

Due to the central role of user in the development of compet-
itive products, user needs and preferences are directly in-
volved in the product life cycle as a standard. The ISO
9241-210:2010—“Human-centred design processes for inter-
active systems” [26] provides requirements and recommenda-
tions for user-centred design principles and activity through-
out the life cycle of software and computer-based interactive
systems, without addressing any specific method or activity.
From this field, the user-centred approach has been

1725

successfully extended to product and services design [27]. In
existing user-centred design methodology, the main activities
are related to the requirement gathering, need assessment, de-
sign for solution and usability evaluation. In [6], a methodol-
ogy with an integrated tool environment that helps designer is
proposed to build effective customer-oriented products. The
tool employs a fuzzy logic approach, to handle the customer’s
linguistic or imprecise demands, which are generally in an
intangible format, in conjunction with a CAD tool, for better
visualization of the product modifications. The use of digital
prototyping for concept validation is proposed in [28], where
users are involved in the conceptual design phase, in three
major validation tasks. The users’ satisfaction on design con-
cepts can be enhanced by making alterations to user needs,
expectations and satisfaction. In [29], a framework for collab-
orative product development and production of modular prod-
ucts is proposed. It focuses on the use of the voice of the
customer (VoC) as external resources to determine a product
solution in order to achieve customer satisfaction and business
goals, in the planning phase. All of the feasible design solu-
tions are generated through a proposed QFD-based procedure,
in order to determine a product solution to achieve customer
satisfaction and business goals. In [30], a design and manage-
rial step-based framework is proposed to help firms achieving
greater success in their efforts to develop new products. The
framework employs already existing methods and techniques,
together with more recent and under development ones, in
order to accomplish the crucial task of properly guiding inno-
vation project team members from idea generation until the
early steps of concept implementation.
The previously described approaches integrate some state-
of-the-art methods involving the user within the early design
phases. The following methods, which are used for translating
user needs into product requirements, will be listed here and
further described in Sect. 3. They are, namely, axiomatic de-
sign [31], Gero’s Function–Behaviour–Structure (FBS)
framework in the extension of Cascini et al. [32], Kansei
Engineering methods [33], quality function deployment [34]
and product attribute function deployment [35]. Similarly, the
decisional process can take into account the user preferences
by means of some of the followings methods: Analytic
Hierarchy Process [36], conjoint analysis [37], decision ma-
trix: Pugh’s method [38], ELECTRE [39], go/no-go screening
[40], feasibility judgment (gut feel) [41], pairwise comparison
[42], preference aggregation models [43], semantic differen-
tial method [44], utility and value theory [45], belief maps
[46], weighted decision matrix [47], method of imprecision
[48] and morphological analysis [49].
Focusing on SMEs, several approaches have been specifi-
cally proposed for involving users in the decision and design
management in SMEs. In [24], the development process of an
effective decision support framework for adopting integrated
information systems within SMEs is presented. The

framework assists SMEs with the decisions they need to make
when considering whether (or not) to integrate their informa-
tion system. The framework helps the SMEs’ practitioners to
identify the most suitable decision support methodology. The
use of the proposed framework leads to support—rather than
replace—decisions based on experience, reduce costs and
risks and improve teamwork between departments. A frame-
work for the collection of the user preferences, for increasing
the user’s satisfaction level, is proposed in [50]. Zeng under-
lines the role of a Web community integrated into a company-
owned platform, used as a mean for innovation. Even if this
approach could be more promising for large companies that
already possess a big community than for SMEs, because a
critical mass has to be reached for codesigning products with
users successfully, SMEs can rely on a closest and more direct
contact to users than larger companies. The authors develop a
conceptual framework illustrating how to integrate such on-
line communities into each stage of a new product develop-
ment process as well as to interconnect them. In [51], a Web
service-based multi-disciplinary collaborative simulation sys-
tem is presented, in order to semiautomatically organize the
CAE software for designing complex products with limited
computing resources. The system is a software-sharing envi-
ronment that allows SMEs to access CAE simulation through
network. The CAE software packages reside in their remote
application program servers. In [52], a methodology for the
NPD procedure is proposed: it consists a set of activities for
NPD for the SMEs’ environment in the plastic processing
industry, which enables the production of products and ser-
vices with a high value added. The developed NPD procedure
consists of five consecutive and overlapping steps: attracting
orders, designing a project, developing a product, developing
a process and zero production series. As main research objec-
tives, the authors identify and validate compatible tools and
methods, which are integrated within the overall NPD proce-
dure. Its implementation delivers good results for project man-
agement in terms of cost-effectiveness and time effectiveness
and particularly good results of long-term process capability.
The paper [53] presents an SME-specific NPD process to
optimize the innovation performance by determining which
activities could be done sequentially, in parallel, or overlap-
ping through a design structure matrix (DSM). The authors
firstly break down the NPD process of some SMEs into indi-
vidual activities by asking the SMEs’ practitioners to name all
relevant activities in their NPD process. Second, they identify
the relations between these activities by asking the respon-
dents in which chronology they normally perform these de-
velopment activities and whether there are any feedback loops
or parallel activities. Then, they match these activities in order
to compare different processes. At the end, after creating an
individual matrix for each of the SMEs’ process, the authors
unify all of them to a final DSM, in order to identify which
activities are independent from each other and can be

1726

performed in parallel. As a result, the developed NPD proce-
dure significantly improves NPD in terms of cost management
and time effectiveness.
Moreover, in the literature as well as on the market, some
examples of software and application are proposed, which are
able to integrate few functions or phases of the product design
process. As an example, in [54], Tu et al. develop a computer-
aided customer interface system for customer requirement
identification and product planning at the early product devel-
opment stages. The customer requirement identification is
based on QFD, while the linear programming technique is
used to support the various personnel to plan their times in
product development. By using this interface, a product de-
velopment team is able to quickly specify a product to meet
customer requirements and needs. Moreover, an optimal
scheduling of the team members’ operations is provided, to
fulfil predefined product technical features and maximizing
the overall customer satisfaction. In [21], an integrated
knowledge-based environment software (named KALIS) is
proposed for enhancing collaboration, knowledge sharing
and information retrieval within the SMEs. The software is
based on three virtual workspaces: a private workspace, which
contains information that can only be accessed by its creator; a
proximity-based workspace, which contains information
shared by a restricted number of engineers within an organi-
zation; a common workspace, which contains public informa-
tion accessible by each organization member.
Among commercial software, most of them are dedicated
only to specific task. As an example, QFDcapture™by
International TechneGroup Incorporated is aimed at translat-
ing the VoC in technical requirements. Other tools integrate
more engineering functions besides requirement management,
as Rational® DOORS® by IBM® Rational® or Cradle® by
3SL: they are user definable and scalable, but each
user/designer requires a software license and needs additional
training to use them.
To sum up, although SMEs could benefit from the applica-
tion of existing methods and tools, an additional effort is re-
quired in order to support the design activities of SMEs by
maximizing the user involvement and contribution. These re-
quirements, according to the research aims of this paper, mo-
tivate our proposal of a framework tailored on SMEs.

3 Methodological framework

A user-centred methodological framework, for the continuous
and iterative interrelation between the user needs and prefer-
ences and the engineering designer perspective during the
whole design process, is proposed.
The framework aims to help the designer in the definition
of the actions related to each design object as well as in the
solution of the design problems related to these actions, by

suggesting appropriate methods. A given set of methods is
provided for each action, in order to allow the designer to
choose the most suitable one, on the basis of his know-how
and on the specific resources available in the SME. However,
the designer is not limited to this set of methods, according to
his expertise. In this way, the designer can tailor his own
design process.
As a novel aspect, this framework involves the user in each
phase of the design process. In particular, the user becomes an
actor in the definition of the product requirements, as well as
in the evaluation of the concept and design alternatives and of
the final prototype.

dressed in this framework are

The proposed framework involves the following list of ac-
tions: elicitation, translation, concept generation, selection,
modelling, visualization, engineering, simulation and optimi-
zation. These actions can be carried out by means of state-of-
the-art methods and techniques, which belong to areas of en-
gineering design or decisional fields. These can be implement-
ed by commercial or free tools, often based on Web applica-
tions (Web tools), or in-house made tools directly developed
by the designers, according to the designer’s intention and
know-how.
In the following, a non-exhaustive collection of the state-
of-the-art methods and techniques used for carrying out the
actions is reported, as a suggestion for the designer in the use
of the framework.

Elicitation As depicted in Fig. 1, the action of elicitation is
applied to achieve the objects’ user needs as well to gather
preferences on the design alternatives and the virtual proto-
type. In the proposed framework, the Web tools for elicitation,
which we identified and selected, belong to conversational
(e.g. questionnaires and surveys, brainstorming, Web forum)
and synthetic (e.g. virtual prototyping, Web scenarios, contex-
tual inquiry) categories. Several Web tools can be found in the
Internet that can be applied without previous specific pro-
gramming languages. As an example, a preliminary selection
of suitable elicitation Web tools and techniques focusing on
the SMEs’ operating context is reported in Fig. 2.

Translation Several methods are focused on the translation of
user needs and preferences into product or technical require-
ments, as listed in Table 1. These methods require the presence
of the user and are usually applied in the early design phases,
seldom being integrated in latter steps of the design process
[35, 55, 56].

Selection The action of selection is applied both to select
concept alternatives in the conceptual design phase and to
design alternatives in the early assessment phase. Methods
related to the action of selection, which involve the user, are
listed in Table 1. Moreover, the fuzzy group decision-making
approaches (e.g. the fuzzy analytic hierarchy process (F-
AHP)) are used to handle uncertainties deriving from the ver-
bal judgments expressed by the decision makers, as in
[57–59].

Concept generation The concept generation action is car-
ried out to generate conceptual design alternatives. To this
aim, several methods can be applied as, for example,
functional analysis [60] and concept combination [61].
The former supports the identification of functional attri-
butes according to the developed requirements. In the lat-
ter, a set of alternatives is generated by means of a com-
bination tool (e.g. the concept combination table, morpho-
logical matrix, classification tree, etc.). In particular, the
morphological method [62, 63] integrates all the previous-
ly described steps, by listing the decomposed functions,
finding conceptual solutions to each function and combin-
ing them into overall concepts.

Modelling The action of modelling is used in the generation
of both conceptual design alternatives and the virtual proto-
type. It is usually carried out by means of parametric 3D CAD
modellers.

Visualization The action of visualization is carried out by the
user to catch the effective shape of the design alternatives and
the virtual prototype, by means of 3D viewer tools, as the
royalty-free cross-platform WebGL. This action supports the
elicitation action.

Engineering The action of engineering is aimed at evalu-
ating the effective performance of the product, in the early
assessment phase. This action is carried out by means of
engineering design-based techniques as design for X [64]
(in which X stands for manufacturing, assembly, environ-
ment, costing, etc.).

On the basis of the design process, the proposed user-
centred design framework involves the phases described in
the following.

3.1 Phase 1: conceptual design phase

In phase 1, three subphases can be distinguished (Fig. 1),
namely the definition of the user requirements, the generation
of conceptual alternatives and the selection decisional phase.
These subphases are hereafter analysed.

3.1.1 Subphase 1A: user need identification and requirement
definition and assessment

This subphase is aimed at gathering the voice of the user
and, subsequentially, at translating user needs into techni-
cal requirements. In particular, an action of elicitation in-
volves the actor user, in order to gather the voice of the
customer and generate the object user needs. Hence, the
actor designer performs an action of translation of the
user needs in order to generate the object’s technical
requirements.

3.1.2 Subphase 1B: concept generation

The objective of this subphase is the definition of conceptual
design alternatives (or concept alternatives). To this aim, the
actor designer performs an action of concept generation.

3.1.3 Subphase 1C: decisional phase

In this subphase, first, the actor user is required to judge on the
concept alternatives, by an action of elicitation. Hence, an
action of selection is performed by both of the actors, user
and designer, to find out a set of the most suitable design
alternatives by means of a decision-making method. The out-
put of subphase 1C is the selected concept object, which has to
be compared to the reference product.

3.2 Phase 2: preliminary design phase

Phase 2 starts from the product requirements, which define the
main features of the selected concept (i.e. the output of phase
1), and ends with the preliminary layout of the product. Three
subphases can be distinguished, as shown in Fig. 1.

3.2.1 Subphase 2A: definition of the advanced concept design

The input data of this subphase consist of a list of product
requirements, which is composed from the technical require-
ments (derived from the subphase 1A) and a set of additional
requirements, derived from regulatory, physics, corporate, etc.
The actor designer performs an action of translation on the
product requirements in order to define a set of design
variables. Hence, the design variables are converted by the
actor designer into a set of design alternatives by means of
an action of modelling, using a 3D CAD modeller.

3.2.2 Subphase 2B: decisional phase (second level)

Starting from the 3D models of the design alternatives, an
action of visualization and, then, an action of elicitation in-
volve the actor user who is required to compare the alterna-
tives. The selection is performed by the actors user and de-
signer, who are decision makers in the selection phase. The
design alternatives are ranked against a set of criteria involv-
ing the requirements in output from subphase 2A, and the
identification of the most suitable design is achieved.

3.2.3 Subphase 2C: definition of the concept preliminary
layout

The actor designer is required to provide the engineering of
the solution identified in the previous subphase, by integrating
manufacturing, economic and environmental (etc.) aspects by
means of specific techniques. The output of this phase is the
object preliminary layout.

3.3 Phase 3: product and process early assessment

The early assessment phase involves the actions of simulation
and optimization performed by the designer, in order to pro-
vide a first product and process performance evaluation as
well as solve criticalities already in the early stage of the prod-
uct development. The output of this phase is a virtual
prototype, which is subjected to a last action of elicitation by
the actor user in order to establish if it complies with prior user
needs.

3.4 Additional features

The proposed framework allows the designer to employ tai-
lored methods for overcoming specific design issues. Among
the existing methods used for translating the user requirements
into technical requirements, quality function deployment
(QFD) [34] is widely used [66, 67] and it is often used in
SMEs [14, 68], even if some limitations have been addressed
in the literature [69, 70], especially related to the use of unnat-

4 Case study

As a case study for the methodological framework, we select-
ed the design of a housing and the related system interfaces of

a biomedical electronic device in collaboration with an elec-
tronic Italian SME.
The SME is selected according to the following criteria: the
SME acts in innovative fields of application and markets,
invests in research and development activities as a source of
continuous improvement and is focused on the development
of new products based on emerging technologies. All of these
criteria require the involvement of users throughout the design
process, so this SME can represent a good test for the frame-
work application. Moreover, the designers’ knowledge back-
ground already includes some state-of-the-art methods and
techniques, but they are inconsistently used within the design
process.
The design of the housing and the related system interfaces
of the biomedical electronic device are based on a printed
circuit board (PCB) with RFID transponder or tag. The device
is aimed to the radiolocation and tracking of patients and per-
sonnel in hospitals, in particular, in maternity and obstetrics
wards. The expected goal is to determine the design attributes
for the housing and the related system interfaces, based on a
PCB advanced layout: only minor modifications are permis-
sible. Due to the peculiar operating conditions, the problem is
characterized by stringent requirements regarding safety, reg-
ulatory and sanitation. Moreover, ergonomics and aesthetics
play a major role in the design process of the housing. Hence,
the hospital personnel of different health institutes have been
involved in the design process as expert users.
Due to the context of usage, this case study is characterized
by requirements that strongly depend on the users, so their
involvement in the whole design process is a key point.
Moreover, due to the different geographical locations of the
users, a communication support is required to enhance the
communication between themselves and between them and
the designers. Similarly, the designers requires a support for
their communication: the design team is composed by an elec-
tronic engineer and a marketing expert, coming from the
SME, and a mechanical engineer, coming from a research
institution. To this aim, the framework includes Web tools that
support the collaboration of all the actors involved, as in the
elicitation and selection steps: the actors can discuss, meet and
share content in real time and visualize images or sketches.
In this section, the framework is detailed by defining—for
each action—the method or technique adopted by the SME’s
design team and the related tool. This information is collected
in two additional columns called “method/technique” and
“tools” beside the columns already presented in Fig. 1, as
shown in the figure describing each phase (Figs. 3, 9 and
11). Finally, the results deriving from the frameworks appli-
cation to the case study are presented.

5 Discussion

The proposed framework is the result of the collaborations we
have experienced with Italian SMEs, belonging to the Emilia–
Romagna region, which are characterized by small-sized en-
terprises, employing less than 50 workers. This context is
characterized by limited financial resources; reduced human
resources, which require employees to simultaneously play
multiple roles; centralized decision-making; short process du-
rations; limited IT and software resources and lack of system-
atic approach in most processes.
Some remarks concerning the application of the proposed
framework will be highlighted. The framework shows to be
effective in the reduction of the design uncertainties, thanks to
the involvement of the user in all the phases of the design

Web tool communication is mainly characterized by absence
of direct contact between users and, particularly, between
users and designers; absence of human interface and media-
tion; use of written language in the description of needs and
preferences and absence of time constraints in answering.
Although the listed characteristics seem to be only disadvan-
tages, on the other hand, they mainly support the objective and
reliability of the collected needs and preferences, due to ano-
nymity, absence of interviewer conditioning and more reflec-
tive answers. Moreover, when required, the Web tools enable
the interaction and communication among the users. Indeed,
direct interactions are substituted with virtual interactions
among users, between users and virtual prototypes or between
users and designers. Regardless from the tools, the main crit-
icalities of elicitation rise in the involvement of the user and in
the interpretation of the user needs and preferences.
Consequently, the presence of a marketing expert in the design
team facilitates the elicitation, but may represent an additional
constraint for the SME. However, the marketing expert could
be an external resource, because the framework enable the
collaboration between design team members located in differ-
ent geographical locations.
Conventional elicitation techniques are substituted with
equivalent virtual techniques through Web tools, without re-
quiring preliminary knowledge, such as specific programming
language. Moreover, conversational techniques, such as brain-
storming and verbal discussions, are substituted with online
brainstorming and Web forums, mostly used in the e-learning
process for idea comparison and internalization, to stimulate
users’ discussion with peers and experts. Moreover, most of
the selected Web tools help in managing the large amount of
information with the automated postprocessing of the collect-
ed data and in providing visual support (as charts and graphs)
to understand trends and statistics. Even if the Web tools
adopted in the proposed framework may work as single-step
tools in the product development, we included them within a
cloud-based service (as a Google cloud-based one), which acts
as a recipe for linking tools, sharing contents and communi-
cating. In particular, it helps users in understanding the con-
cepts in their complete development; allows them to visualize
and manipulate the CAD data, with a medium level of cus-
tomization possibility and enables the designers (and expert

users) to support users in understanding their real needs and
their prioritization.
The framework integrates state-of-the-art methods and
techniques, in order to face specific issues, such as in the
translation of the user needs into product requirements and
in the selection decisional phase. A disadvantage concerning
the application of the framework may be related to the actual
designer knowledge on the design and decisional methods,
which could affect the efficiency of the framework.
Sometimes, a previous training on the methods would be re-
quired for the designers.
Some considerations related to the methods used in the case
study could be pointed out. In the translation of user needs into
product requirements, QFD is widely used, also in SMEs, due
to its ease of use, even if affected by some limitations. The
proposed NBFD aims to overcome the drawback of the im-
portance attribution to each user need. The quantitative score
formulated for each need, based on the number of users that
consider that need as important for the product, reveals to be
more robust to subjectivism. In the traditional QFD, the de-
signer shows difficulty in interpreting the user’s real needs,
generally due to the user difficulty in understanding the met-
rics of judgement and due to the subjective weight that each
user assigns the same scores, both numerical and fuzzy [71,
72]. Even if the fuzzy approach tends to better decode the real
needs of users, the use of the NBFD allows a further simpli-
fication of the judgment process by the user. In the NBFD, the
designer asks each user to indicate his most important needs
without assigning scoring or ranking, so the difficulty in
decoding the users’ feedback is overcame. In this way, no
preference aggregation techniques are adopted, maintaining
the individuality of each user preference. Moreover, the
NBFD is capable to distinguish preferences based on socio-
demographical or geographical characteristics of the sample
of users. As a disadvantage, the user assigns importance only
to p needs among the m needs previously identified. The num-
ber p, which ranges between one third to one half m based on
the designer’s experience, may force the user to leave out one
or more needs considered as important.
In the decisional phases of the proposed framework, a
fuzzy approach is adopted to face the lack of reliability or lack
of awareness that may affect the user judgments. In order to
describe the user preferences, the use of a verbal judgment
may overcome the difficulties in assigning a score for a qual-
itative judgment or a rank between options. Some limitations
may be represented by lack of reliability of the judgements, in
case of a small sample of users. The group F-AHP, which has
been applied here for group decision-making purposes, uses a
weighting approach to deal with the judgment of three deci-
sion makers. Since these judgments have to be aggregated to
find a unique mean value, the major limitation is related to the
choice of suitable preference aggregation functions. Further
considerations are in [57]. A predefined in-house made

6 Conclusions

This paper presents a systematic user-centred framework that
is able to face the specific issues affecting SMEs, as highlight-
ed in the Sects. 1 and 2. The framework systematically sup-
ports the SMEs’ design team in the solution of design prob-
lems by integrating the user’s viewpoint and the designer’s
perspective, throughout all phases of the design process. The
framework proposes a flow of objects, defines actions in each
phase of the design process, ensures the involvement of users
when required and accordingly suggests appropriate method-
ological approaches. Starting from the analysis of the wide
state-of-the-art and scientific literature, a portfolio of methods
and techniques is proposed and integrated in the framework as
a suitable trade-off between ease of use, efficiency and accu-
racy of the results. The designer is not forced in using specific
methods or techniques, but he can choose them among a sug-
gested portfolio for tailoring his own design process, on the
basis of his know-how and the SMEs’ specific resources.
In accordance to the target of supporting the development
of new industrial products in SMEs, the framework has
proved to be flexible, accessible and easy to use. The frame-
work is capable of integrating well-known and new tech-
niques, tailored on the practitioners’ know-how; it presents a
reduced demand of resources, in terms of money, software,
time mainly due to the use of Web tools and tailor-made tools.
Moreover, it enhances the product development process,
avoiding latter redesign activities or major errors in the intro-
duction of ill-conceived products in the market and reducing
the time to market.
The proposed systematic framework assures to enlarge the
sample dimension of users, both in collecting needs and pref-
erences; shorten responses time of users, both in collecting
needs and preferences and act by means of Web tools includ-
ing virtual prototypes and scenarios.
The framework is tested on the development of the housing
and the related system interfaces of a biomedical electronic
device, to be worn by hospital patients and personnel. Due to
the particular contest of usage, the use of elicitation Web tools
allows to involve personnel from different hospitals as lead
users, for supporting the conceptual design process. In partic-
ular, online surveys and questionnaires, Web forums, virtual
prototypes and scenario elicitation tools have been integrated
in the design process. As a result, the developed housing has
been produced in a limited preseries.
The case study selected for this paper deals with the devel-
opment of a single product: in the future, the framework will
be applied to an industrial configurable product, in order to

further assess the framework in a more complex context and in
relation with different categories of users.
As a further development, for enhancing the usability and
the accessibility of the framework by the user, all the tools
related to methods and techniques involving the users will
be implemented in a single-page application. In this way, these
tools (e.g. elicitation tools, visualization tools, chat and forum,
etc.) will be concurrently displayed and made accessible.
