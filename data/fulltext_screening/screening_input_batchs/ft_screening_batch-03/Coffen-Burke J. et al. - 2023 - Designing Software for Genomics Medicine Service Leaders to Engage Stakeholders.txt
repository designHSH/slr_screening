II. 
DESIGN MOTIVATION 
The primary design motivation was to enable stakeholder 
engagement around deciding what types of genomic medicine 
services and genetic test offerings to support at an institution. If 
we consider implementing genomic medicine services more 
broadly as a means for quality improvement, the importance of 
clinician stakeholder involvement is well-recognized [8]. 
Clinical champions with relevant experiences can help other 
healthcare providers become aware of genomic medicine 
services and genetic test offerings, and of how they might be 
used in their practice [9]. Such clinical stakeholders can provide 
a positive perception of the services and options, identify points 
of potential resistance, and leverage their influence to overcome 
resistance in clinical settings given their frequent interactions 
with other clinical professionals on the frontline [10]. Potential 
reasons for a lack of engagement include a perception of the cost 
of change exceeding the anticipated benefit [11] and a conflict 
in leadership priorities or values set by physicians [12]. 
Furthermore, disengagement can occur due to a non-supportive 
practice culture or organizational structure [13].  

Organizational readiness for change is one mechanism by which 
stakeholder engagement may lead to an improved likelihood of 
a successful genomic medicine service. Organizational

commitment to change and ability to implement the change [14]. 
According to the theory on organizational readiness, quality 
improvement is most likely to occur when individuals feel 
committed to the change and perceive that they will have the 
ability to implement that change [14].  

Drawing from that theory, there are important considerations for 
the design of software to enable stakeholder engagement: (1) 
facilitating key stakeholder review and endorsement prior to 
pursuing a new genomic medicine service or genetic test 
offering can improve the likelihood of its success, and (2) 
seeking the opinions and feedback of key stakeholders on 
genomic medicine services and genetic test offerings early in the 
process can lead to those stakeholders becoming service 
partners that are both committed to it and perceive it can be 
successfully implemented at their organization. 

III. 
METHODS 

Our human-centered design process was driven by a modified 
design sprint methodology [16]. Main research activities 
included 
participatory 
design 
sessions, 
semi-structured 
interviews with experts on institutional review and approval 
processes for new genetic test offerings provided by a genomic 
medicine (GM) service team, and end-user testing sessions with 
institutional leaders that propose and implement such offerings 
(i.e., GM service leaders). 

A. Participatory Design Sessions 
From mid-October 2022 to mid-January 2023, we held design 
sessions in which we integrated a modified design sprint 
methodology. The design sprint process includes a series of 
user-centered design activities and assigns a specific schedule to 
complete those activities in five consecutive days [16]. Similar 
to others seeking to apply this methodology in a research setting 
[17], we made several adaptations. In summary, we scheduled 
specific periods for team members to attend design sessions and 
added time to organize, reflect on, and verify findings from 
expert consultations. We also delayed end-user testing to 
establish and internally test a semi-structured interview guide. 
Fig. 1 illustrates the main design sprint steps including: (1) 
mapping out paths to a solution, (2) sketching potential new 
solutions, (3) deciding which sketched solutions to pursue 
further and creating a storyboard, (4) turning the storyboard into 
a prototype, and (5) testing the prototype with end users. 

 
Fig. 1. Overview of modified design sprint steps. 

 
We used participatory design sessions to share empirical 
findings from expert consultations at the first mapping step and 
following the fifth end-user testing step. After the mapping step, 
during our session, we spent time reflecting on findings from 
expert consultations to revise our map and verify the map prior 
to the next stage of the design process. Following the 
prototyping step, we also spent time creating and iterating on the 
prototype and a semi-structured interview protocol prior to 
testing.

C. Building Prototype System Design and Functionality 
Figma (figma.com) was used to design and build a low-fidelity, 
clickable prototype based on the core system functionalities and 
the role of potential users. We focused our content examples on 
genetic test offerings to bring pharmacogenomics-guided drug 
therapy into use clinically. In this context, pharmacogenomics 
can be used as a clinical tool to assist in drug selection and 
dosing and reduce the risk for drug-related problems.  

Pharmacogenomic (PGx) tests are particularly relevant because 
they may require more strategies to engage stakeholders than 
disease-focused genetic tests due to a need for more extensive 
infrastructure to integrate into routine care [6]. Content 
presented in the prototype included three metrics described in 
the literature as important areas to provide evidence around 
pharmacogenomic testing: drug-related problems, economic 
value, and start-up effort.  We included drug-related problems 
as a metric given recognition from groups such as the Federal 
Drug Administration that adverse drug reactions can be 
prevented with pre-emptive genetic testing [18]. Economic 
value was included given the importance of having accurate 
estimates of costs and use of health services [19]. Start-up effort 
was included in recognition that there can be pre-requisites for 
the adoption of a genetic test e.g., improving genetic test 
turnaround time [20]. 

To create role-specific semi-structured interview protocols, end-
user tasks and content-related questions were initially created by 
JCB, ZL, KY and piloted with the entire team during a 
participatory design session. Following that session, revisions to 
the prototype and the interview protocol were made.

GM service leaders were interviewed and asked to complete

intended to last 45-60 minutes in duration. To allow for better 
recall at the data analysis stage, after each session we created a 
summary of the main points, key findings, and the repetition of 
keywords, ideas, and phrases used by the participant. 

E. Evaluation Guided by Design Considerations 
We analyzed notes taken during end-user sessions for themes. 
After we completed data collection for each end user group, we 
used a thematic data analysis approach [21], [22] to analyze the 
notes collected under three main topics: issues/challenges, 
recommendations, and likes. Notes under each task were 
assigned initial codes for themes and sub-themes by one of two 
reviewers, and a second reviewer indicated agreement or 
disagreement with each code. Disagreements were resolved 
during group discussions and consensus was reached on the final 
coded themes and sub-themes. We maintained a detailed 
description of the user feedback gathered from the notes for the 
sub-theme assignments.  

A final count of the unique sub-themes associated with user 
feedback from the user testing session was calculated. To 
synthesize our results, we used an online whiteboard called Miro 
(Miro.com) [23] to create affinity maps for each sub-theme. The 
affinity mapping allowed us to organize, define and summarize 
details covered by each sub-theme in a visual way and to quickly 
assess the overall details of each sub-theme. The outcome of this

IV. 
RESULTS 

A. Map Following Expert Consultations 
For our initial map, we sought to describe the interface between 
various oversight review committees responsible for the 
approval of a new genetic test offering. We thus interviewed 
four experts that currently or previously participated in clinical 
decision support (CDS) review committees and/or pharmacy 
and therapeutic committees. Findings from those consultations 
clarified that process and allowed us to revise our map to better 
reflect current processes toward offering a new genetic test. In 
addition, all four experts suggested stakeholder engagement as 
an upstream requirement to introducing a genetic test offering to 
any institutional oversight committee. Our design goal thus 
focused on that need.

V. 
DISCUSSION 

Our software design has the potential to improve the likelihood 
of implementing successful genetic test offerings, by facilitating 
key stakeholder review and endorsement of an offering. The 
level of endorsement for a genetic test offering may help GM 
service providers decide whether or not to move it through the 
institutional approval process. Our human-centered design 
strategy used a modified design sprint methodology. At the first 
step in our process, we found that taking time to reflect on 
findings from expert consultations helped us to revise our map 
to a solution. The revised map was then used to guide early 
mockups of the review and prioritization software and develop 
the low-fidelity prototype.  

Across all tasks, user testing feedback indicated the following 
top three sub-themes: add new information, general approval, 
and add/change functionality. The second-rated sub-theme, 
general approval (48 comments, 4 synthesized comments) 
illustrates overall approval and satisfaction with the review and 
prioritization design and process. However, the other top-rated 
sub-themes add new information (92 comments, 14 synthesized 
comments) and add/change functionality (36 comments, 8 
synthesized comments) identified areas of improvement to

Findings from reviewing the general approval subtheme

a clear and intuitive process. The subthemes related to clinical

flexibility for GM service leaders to select the individuals they 
believe are appropriate to review the evidence and to provide 
endorsement for a proposed genetic test offering.  

The add new information subtheme highlighted a desire to 
include the credentials of individuals and whether they have 
genomic medicine or pharmacogenomics expertise. Other 
desired information was around the potential to engage or 
champion a new genetic test offering (e.g., an indicator of 
individuals who had been contacted for feedback previously and 
had completed a review). Such data could be tracked by the 
software and pulled into the search functionality. We are able to 
include more details about individuals, though there may be 
some details that would be missing by default. If implemented, 
these enhancements would help system users to make more 
informed stakeholder selections based on level of experience 
and areas of expertise. Feasibility to provide the most accurate 
and up-to-date information in requested areas, however, requires 
further investigation. Individuals, for example, may change 
career roles, employment, expertise, and location which can 
create challenges to maintaining such up-to-date information.

B. Design Consideration #2: Train and Educate Stakeholders. 
The review form that a GM service leader creates and distributes 
to selected stakeholders was used to implement this design 
consideration. Content included in the review form would both 
introduce stakeholders to a proposed genetic test offering and 
provide educational information about the service. For our 
study, we showed sample content in the prototype for one PGx 
genetic test offering: CYP2C19 Genotype for Choice of 

Antiplatelet Therapy. The content was presented under three 
metrics: drug-related problems, economic value, and start-up 
effort (Fig. 3). Similar to design consideration #1, we found 
several comments related to content-related subthemes (add new 
information and confusing terminology, 8 synthesized 
comments), subthemes that provide positive feedback (general 
approval and liked features, 6 synthesized comments), and 
subthemes related to clinical stakeholders (unclear of clinicians 
as targeted key stakeholders and add stakeholders, 3 
synthesized comments). There were also comments under the 
add/change functionality subtheme (3 synthesized comments). 

GM service leader interviewees provided positive feedback on 
the process to prepare content in the review form, on the 
information that was provided, the simplicity and conciseness of 
that information, and the visual characteristics of the form. 
There was also general approval of the resources we cited, 
including published articles and CPIC guidelines. The 
add/change functionality and content-related themes indicated a 
preference to add new information and some areas where the 
terminology used was confusing. Most areas for new 
information can be addressed with our current design since it is 
under user control (e.g., adding links to other evidence 
resources, more information on cost considerations and clinical 
impact, including more quantitative data about risk and cost-
effectiveness, etc.). Other areas for new information and 
functionality could be supported by providing access to local 
data (e.g., interviewees indicated a desire for summaries on 
drug-related problems derived from electronic health record 
data). Feedback also indicated a need to improve how 
information is structured to improve readability and to enable 
quick assessment of a metric. Flexible format options should be 
supported to address this need. Furthermore, there was a need to 
support more customized content with one comment indicating 
a desire to edit and change what metrics are presented. The one 
confusing terminology comment raised could also be addressed 
by enabling more customization in the review form. The

illustrated that GM service providers also need to compile local 
evidence for a new genetic test offering, and that evidence may 
be different from what is shared and reviewed by the selected 
stakeholders. Beyond the comments related to this design 
consideration, we found that the PRECISE-Value model was 
liked by most of our participants and may address this need for 
local evidence. To support decisions to move a genetic test 
offering forward, we should consider introducing such a model 
earlier in the user process. The current design introduced 
PRECISE-Value after the review summary. More work is 
needed to understand data access requirements to incorporate 
such a tool into the user process. 

In sum, findings illustrated a need for designs such as what was 
shown in the response summary page is appropriate to support 
the work that GM service leaders do to synthesize feedback from 
stakeholders. We also found, however, that there is a need to 
improve our initial design so that the submissions made by 
stakeholders are more clearly summarized, and so that local 
evidence can be included in a report along with the stakeholder 
review summary. Doing this is a critical step toward our broader 
goal to enable prioritizing new genetic test offerings and 
decisions to move them through institutional approval 
processes. 

D. Limitations and Future Work 
 
First, we performed our analysis with a sample size of seven GM 
service leaders. While this is a small number, this is within ai 
sample size that is common for qualitative, user experience, and 
participatory design research [26]. Second, many of the 

synthesized comments were not relevant to the design 
considerations of focus in this work (39%, N=54). Other 
synthesized comments will be used to identify lower-priority 
design improvements. Third, we were unable to study the 
relationship 
between 
stakeholder 
engagement 
and 
organizational readiness for a GM service or genetic test 
offering. The relationship between stakeholder engagement and 
quality improvement projects is supported by empirical findings 
of others [27], but more work remains to empirically study this 
relationship with GM services specifically. Future work will 
study the relationship between stakeholder engagement and GM 
service and genetic test adoption. Furthermore, future work is 
required to establish the viability of our software design in the 
context of a range of GM services and genetic test offerings 
(including PGx and disease-focused GM services and genetic 
testing).
