ABSTRACT

The integration of robots into our daily lives is progressing steadily.
Vacuum cleaner robots, for example, will already represent a very
extensive market segment in 2024. A further step in the near future
will involve the use of humanoid robots, for example, in the office,
in restaurant or café service, and in elderly care.
In this paper, we present a comprehensive user-centered design
approach for the development of social robots in the office environ-
ment. User needs, technical requirements, specific user interactions,
as well as environmental and human factors are considered in
an appropriate way without raising exaggerated expectations or
disregarding possible fears. We present an in-depth methodical de-
scription of the design process, its practical application, and initial
findings for two specific scenarios. Additionally, we identified key
challenges that are highly relevant for practical use in the office
environment.

INTRODUCTION

In recent years, the use of robots to collaborate and interact with
humans has become more and more widespread. Mopping and vac-
uuming robots are now commonplace in many households. In the
foreseeable future, the integration of social assistive robots (SAR)
into our daily lives will be a permanent feature and herald a new
era in which humans and robots coexist and interact seamlessly
in everyday scenarios. They promise to make our lives easier, and
become a constant companion without overwhelming us. Further-
more, the integration of SARs into office environments is currently
considered a promising approach in the field of workplace innova-
tion. With advances in robotics technology, the potential of SARs
to enhance productivity, foster collaboration, and contribute to a
positive office environment has gained substantial attention. For
example, front-desk robots, equipped with advanced artificial in-
telligence and interactive capabilities, may emerge as innovative
solutions to streamline administrative tasks, improve visitor experi-
ences, and contribute to overall operational efficiency. In reception-
less offices, these robots serve as digital ambassadors, welcoming
and assisting guests with check-ins, providing information, and
even directing them to their destinations.
In order to best tailor the use of robots to the needs, experiences
and possible fears of users and employees in office environments,
users must be involved in the development process from the very
beginning. User-Centered Design (UCD), a paradigm rooted in
understanding and addressing user needs, preferences, and behav-
iors, becomes particularly crucial when introducing SARs to the

45

workplace. Unlike traditional industrial robots confined to manu-
facturing settings, SARs navigate dynamic, human-centric office
environments, necessitating an approach that prioritizes user expe-
rience and seamless integration.
This research contributes to a better understanding of the use
and integration of SARs in office environments. Furthermore, we
want to better understand the actual needs of users and analyze
the contextual influences that must be considered when designing
such robots. To gain these deep insights, we follow a user-centered
perspective that emphasizes the user’s perspective through an inter-
active design process that involves and engages the user from start
to finish [2]. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows.
Section 2 discusses related work. Section 3 provides the theoret-
ical background and a detailed description of the various phases
and steps of our envisioned design process. In Section 4 we show
the practical application of the design process and present first
results and findings. The paper concludes with a discussion of key
challenges in Section 5 and a summary in Section 6.

RELATED WORK
2.1
User-Centered Design of Robots

As robots become increasingly integrated into people’s lives, it’s
crucial to understand how individuals will interact with these robots
in a given context [27]. One approach to gain this understanding
is through UCD. In UCD, users are involved in identifying design
needs, providing feedback, and evaluating solutions throughout the
process [2].
Examples of applied UCD processes in the context of robotics
can be found in early work, such as [25, 36], or more recently in [67].
Pollmann and Ziegler argue in [45] that Human-Robot Interaction
(HRI) needs to be personalized in order to be truly social. They
propose a methodological framework for developing personalized
HRI with a specific focus on personalization based on the tradi-
tional UCD process. The process focuses on obtaining information
about the users’ characteristics and needs to create matching design
variants. The importance of individual and autonomic adaptation
of technological systems to people’s abilities to ensure safe, healthy,
and productive work is also emphasized in [17]. Darriba Frederiks
et al. argue that there is a rich and evolving set of UCD tools in
human-computer interaction. To promote the use of UCD tech-
niques in the design of SARs as well, they proposed Opsoro, a do-it-
yourself platform to enable the creation of SARs with non-experts
[13]. Opsoro includes an open-source programming, hardware, and
embodiment framework that focuses on providing simple means
for prototyping to go from character concept to a functional SAR.
Such frameworks could especially be integrated in the prototyping
phase of a UCD approach (cf. Sec. 3.2.4).

2.2
Front-Desk Robots

Front-desk robots, as well as service robots in general, are attracting
increasing interest and are gradually being integrated into frontline
services [34]. An important feature of these robots is their ability
not only to provide technological functions for services, but also
to participate socially in human interactions [7]. A general defini-
tion and overview of the different roles of service robots has been
proposed in [63].

Service robots are increasingly being used and explored in the
hotel and hospitality industries. For example, the Henn-na Hotel,
operating in Nagasaki, was the first hotel where all employees were
robots [40]. Tussyadiah and Park investigated guests’ perceptions
of robot concierge services and their adoption intentions [59]. Their
research focused on the use cases of 1) check-in and 2) room delivery
services. They found that the acceptance is significantly influenced
by the anthropomorphism, perceived intelligence and safety of
the service robot. Additionally, they noted that users’ attention is
mainly focused on the robot’s face, with less attention paid to other
parts of its body. Gardecki et al. present an example of the use of
the humanoid robot Pepper for reception tasks [18]. The robot’s
capabilities were extended to provide a fully automated reception
system that can interact naturally with the user via speech. The
challenges identified relate to the physical adaptations of the robot’s
workplace and the robot itself. This suggests that the context of use
and the eventual work environment should be considered early in
the design. Other examples of robot receptionists outside the hotel
industry can be found in [3, 54]. Both robots represent medical
receptionists that interact with patients. Although perceived as
friendly, users did not trust the robots to handle complex tasks and
situations, and human assistance should be available if needed.
Despite promising results, there are also barriers and challenges
that need to be addressed to drive adoption forward. Wang et al.
examined the consumer resistance to service robots at hotel front
desks and identified barriers such as the risk of malfunction, com-
plexity, and unattractive appearance, among others [61]. A study
was conducted on the general perception of robots as employees of
the hotel industry [64]. This study also revealed negative attitudes
towards human-like robots. This is often due to the fact that users
have higher expectations of the capabilities of human-like robots
compared to machine-like ones [39]. Furthermore, according to
the Uncanny Valley theory [38], human-like robots that appear
too realistic can evoke negative perceptions [53]. These are crucial
aspects that should already be taken into account at the design
stage in order to ensure the necessary user acceptance and trust.

2.3
Collaborative Robotics

Collaborative robotic systems, also known as cobots [44, 55], have
developed rapidly in recent years and have become a prominent area
in industry and manufacturing. Cobots are designed to interact and
co-operate with human workers in a shared workspace to perform
tasks safely and efficiently. In line with the paradigms of Industry
4.0 and Industry 5.0, separate work areas for humans and robotic
systems can therefore be increasingly abandoned and transformed
into a cooperative workplace. In addition to increasing efficiency,
the main focus is on relieving the burden on human factory workers,
for example, to support them when transporting heavy loads (e.g.,
[14, 49]) or generally to provide the best possible protection from
health hazards (e.g., [4, 50]).
By integrating advanced sensor technology for environmental
and situational awareness, safe physical interaction with humans
is enabled. This sensor technology is the most central aspect in the
realisation of safe collaboration between humans and machines
and is generally based on a combination of multiple technologies
[43]. Force and torque sensors are essential for safe human-machine

interaction, as they allow the robot to recognize when a collision oc-
curs or excessive force is applied [35]. Despite their quick response
times, it’s important to note the potential risks associated with
impulse transfer during collisions or when handling sharp tools
[65]. Vision-based sensor systems (e.g., cameras, lidar and infrared
sensors) capture optical data from the environment to recognise
objects, enable safe navigation, and help avoiding collisions (e.g.,
[32, 47]). Ultrasonic sensor technology is often used as a supple-
ment to imaging systems and can help with the early and real-time
detection of obstacles, especially in situations where the view block-
age for the cameras makes it difficult to recognise obstacles [37]. In
general, machine learning and AI techniques have improved the
autonomous decision-making and adaptability of these systems
and also improved their ability to predict and understand human
intentions and interactions to form more effective and natural col-
laboration. For example, the latest advances include robust voice
commands [58], natural text communication [46], visual emotion
recognition [28], and realistic emotion renderings [26].
Furthermore, standards and regulations to ensure safety and
data protection are still important aspects in the implementation
of collaborative assistance systems. Besides malfunctions, extrin-
sic manipulation of the robotic systems (e.g., remote control by
hackers to provoke damage) is a central security problem [1]. In
addition, sensitive data (images and language of users) and implicit
surveillance of the environment [8, 21] must also be implemented
in compliance with human privacy rights and the General Data
Protection Regulation1. It is precisely here that legal regulations,
for example, in the use of facial recognition software, are slow-
ing down the currently unlimited growth of improvements in the
field of AI (e.g., the EU AI Act restricting use of face recognition
technology[42]).

3
METHODOLOGY

In this section, we provide the theoretical background and a detailed
description of the various phases and steps of our design process.
The chosen design methodology is based on UCD paradigms, en-
riched with aspects of participatory design [51] to involve future
users in the design process (e.g., exploratory prototyping with cre-
ative drawing). The described methodology provides an example of
a structured approach that can be used to gain a deep user under-
standing and, consequently, to design and develop SARs. It should
be noted that our proposed design methodology does not require
the presence of a robot while performing these steps. On the con-
trary, the results of these steps can be used to make an informed
decision about whether to buy a commercially available robot or to
build your own robot platform from scratch. Below you will find
detailed instructions on the individual steps so that they can be
easily adopted by others.

3.1
Principles

We define a set of principles that guide the design process described
in the following.

3.1.1
Scenario-based Exploration. Instead of relying on a fully user-
informed design process, we use a scenario-based exploration ap-
proach to focus the areas of interest based on our goals as a basis
for structuring the exploration in our design process. Similar princi-
ples have been proposed in [41, 57]. We believe that this approach
allows for a more focused and nuanced design process as the team
can delve deeply into the details of specific user interactions, envi-
ronmental and human factors, and user needs associated with the
chosen scenarios, and thus aligned with real-world situations. This
approach may ultimately lead to more effective and contextually
relevant design outcomes.

3.1.2
Divergent and Convergent Thinking. Divergent and conver-
gent thinking are two essential forms of creative thought [12],
which was originally described in [6]. The divergent phase is char-
acterized by the generation of multiple ideas and concepts. Its
purpose (e.g., through prototyping [56]) is to foster a wide array
of alternatives and solutions, and thus encourages exploration and
creativity. The convergent phase is about focusing and narrowing
down the ideas and concepts generated in the divergent phase. This
involves critical thinking and decision-making to refine and stream-
line the ideas and provoke the thinking process towards actionable
and practical solutions. By alternating between these phases, we
can ensure that the final solutions are both practical and aligned
with the user’s needs. Divergent and convergent thinking is already
successfully used in the context of HRI-Design [5, 41].

3.1.3
Mixed Design Tools and Activities. In the realm of designing
a SAR, recognizing the diverse ways users express their thoughts
is important. Offering a varied set of design tools and activities
aligns with the multifaceted nature of human communication, fos-
tering creativity and engagement. By accommodating different
modes of thinking, whether visual, verbal, or tactile, the design
process becomes enriched, ensuring that the SAR resonates with
users across various preferences. Providing diverse communica-
tion channels allows users to articulate their thoughts and ideas
effectively, contributing to a more inclusive and comprehensive
design. Moreover, integrating tactile, hands-on activities, such as
drawing or other interactive methods, injects a tangible element
into the design process. This not only stimulates creativity but also
facilitates a deeper connection between users and the envisioned
SAR, ultimately enhancing the overall user experience.

3.2
Process

The main techniques adopted in our design process are interviews,
observations, user narratives, and scenarios, which all resemble typ-
ical methods used in a UCD process [52]. Fig. 1 provides an overview
of the individual process steps and sessions. All steps should in-
volve a collaborative effort from a diverse, inter-disciplinary team,
comprising, for example, designers, developers, researchers, and
roboticists, to ensure a common understanding among all stake-
holders. The practices also resonate with well-known principles of
the Lean UX philosophy [19] or the Contextual Design methodol-
ogy [24]. In the following, we give a detailed description of each
step and its expected outcome.

3.2.3
Contextual Data Analysis. In the data analysis step, the hand-
written raw notes and recorded material are reviewed and trans-
formed into work activity notes during so-called interpretation
sessions. Work activity notes describe a meaningful, elemental con-
cept, idea, fact or theme in the form of a statement that emerges
from the research data [23]. We recommend that these sessions are
scheduled close in time to the data collection dates. This enables

the utilization of fresh memories while reviewing the notes. The
resulting work activity notes can then be used to form insights
by building so-called design-informing models (or, in a more tra-
ditional setting, to extract formal requirements). This can include,
for example, a thematic analysis to identify overarching themes
within the data [10], empathy maps (to understand the users’ mind-
set), user journey maps (to understand processes and interactions),
among others. Based on our experience, we suggest utilizing user
personas and user narratives for effectively integrating and sum-
marizing data. These models not only facilitate communication in
the broader context but also convey key ideas within the team and
to stakeholders.
Outcome: Work Activity Notes, Design-informing models

3.2.4
Exploratory Prototyping. Exploratory prototyping involves
the swift creation and testing of initial iterations of the design
concept, aiming to refine the design-informing models from the data
analysis step. In this step, we focus on better understanding user
needs, placing a strong emphasis on creativity, experimentation, and
exploration to generate insights and initial ideas. This may begin
with a brainstorming session, where initial ideas are generated
to address users’ issues or pain points. Moreover, in this step we
also recommend considering first ethical and privacy impacts and
risks, which become increasingly relevant in the design of robotic
applications [48, 60]. This can be achieved by using low-fidelity
prototypes, such as sketches or cardboard prototypes. Cardboard
prototyping serves as a cost-effective and versatile method for
designing and testing social robots in office environments. The
use of cardboard allows for rapid iteration and modification of
robot designs, enabling researchers and developers to visualize and
refine key features before investing in more expensive materials
or the real robot. This approach facilitates an early exploration of
the robot’s physical form, interaction capabilities, and overall user
experience. Additionally, cardboard prototypes are valuable tools
for soliciting feedback by using methods such as questionnaires,
interviews and focus groups with potential users and stakeholders,
providing insights into the social acceptability and practicality of
introducing robots into office settings.
Outcome: Prototypes, Revised design-informing models

4
RESULTS AND FINDINGS

In the following, we describe our findings on how social robots can
be used effectively in an office environment. We provide a high-
level protocol of the applied design methodology which is described
in Sec. 3. The process steps and various sessions were carried out
over a period of approximately seven months in the context of an
academic institution. The University of Applied Sciences Upper
Austria, with its more than 5500 students, is spread over five dif-
ferent locations, which makes administration a major challenge.
A local administration on each campus has to work together with
a central administration at the head office. The head office is re-
sponsible for management, global human resources, accounting
and controlling, among other things. The 91 bachelor’s and mas-
ter’s programs together require a great deal of administrative effort,
because about a third of them are organized as part-time studies. In
addition, the administration is spread over many different buildings
and floors, making it difficult for first-time or infrequent visitors

to quickly find the right person or location. All quotations and
technical terms have been translated from German to English.

4.1
Scenario Definition

Based on an initial discussion with stakeholders, a collaborative
effort was undertaken to identify situations and research questions
in the context of office environments that could benefit from the ap-
plication of socially assistive robots. Based on initial insights from
these discussions and a first look at the related work in the litera-
ture (see Sec. 2), we formulated two scenarios to guide subsequent
activities:
Scenario 1: Office. The daily routine in an office is often char-
acterized by standardized and recurring tasks. Despite ongoing
digitization, one central activity is the transportation of artifacts,
such as mail and other documents. The forthcoming development
of a mobile robot platform aims to improve these activities. This
system can be utilized for specialized pick-and-deliver services,
assisting employees in streamlining their workdays and overall
help saving time on recurring tasks, thus providing a valuable tool
for optimizing efficiency and productivity.
Scenario 2: Concierge. The reception area is often a bustling
space, where staff deal with a variety of requests and questions
from different people, such as visitors and service providers. To meet
these demands, an embodied digital assistant is proposed to support
and relieve employees in the reception area by interacting with
visitors, answering questions and providing information. The aim
is to promote a seamless and natural interaction between humans
and machines, improving the overall efficiency and user experience
in the reception space.

4.2
Ethnographic Interviews

In this particular context of the defined application scenario within
office environments, the project team members (eight individuals,
all male) could themselves be considered domain experts due to
their own day-to-day experiences and insights as described in [22].
However, to gather additional perspectives and gain a broader un-
derstanding of the issues at hand, four ethnographic interviews
were conducted with four individuals who occupy different roles
within the organization. This approach aimed to enrich the project’s
findings by incorporating diverse viewpoints and ensuring a com-
prehensive understanding of the challenges faced.
All participants (P1, P2, P3, P4) in the interviews were female
and their ages were grouped: P1 and P2 are aged between 41-50,
P3 is under 21, and P4 is aged between 31-40. Their job title and
role in the organization varied, with P1 working as an assistant to
the management, P2 as an assistant to a department head, P3 as
an employee of the human resources team and P4 as a team leader
in the payroll department. Furthermore, the participants have a
good mix of tenure in their respective roles within the organization,
providing a good balance of experience and fresh perspectives. P1
has been in her current role for 20 years, P4 for 8 years, while
P2 and P3 have been in their roles for 3 and 2 years, respectively.
Their working hours range from 30 to 40 hours per week. We also
assessed the participants’ affinity for technology interaction using
the ATI scale [16]. In this aspect as well, we see a wide range, with

scores ranging from 2.11 (P1) to 4.56 (P4), and an overall M = 3.56,

All interviews were audio recorded. In addition, important sit-
uations and artifacts were photographed. In total, we collected
around 04:30 hours of audio, around 35 photographs, and 18 pages

4.3
Contextual Data Analysis

The material collected in the ethnographic interviews was reviewed,
interpreted, and transformed into so-called work activity notes, as
described in Sec. 3. This resulted in a total of 149 notes generated,
which formed the basis for all further data analysis steps described
in this section.

4.3.1
Thematic Analysis. As the interviews covered a wide range
of topics (including workplace, daily routines, processes and tasks,
etc.), we used a thematic analysis [9] to identify common themes
and topics within the dataset. For this purpose, we have summarized
the findings along these key themes:
Work Environment and Workspace. Participants generally value
their workplace, with some preferring the quiet and privacy of a
small office (e.g., a two-person office) to an open-plan office. They
value the ability to personalize their workspace and office equip-
ment (some use a standing desk). However, they also note that in-
terruptions (so-called time thieves, e.g., answering phone calls and
requests from visitors, delivery services or colleagues) can hinder
their concentration and workflow and can require a considerable
amount of time to regain focus.
Task and Document Management. Participants find that certain
tasks, such as running recurring reports, maintaining customer
(CRM) data, and processing emails, are monotonous. They also
note that tasks that depend on other colleagues or departments can
be time-consuming. Some use applications (e.g., MS Outlook) to
manage their tasks (e.g., set reminders or mark to-do items), while
others suggest the need for a comprehensive system to monitor
open tasks. Participants also expressed a desire to automate certain
repetitive tasks, such as producing quarterly reports, requesting
parking tickets for visitors, and managing templates for recurring
emails. In terms of documents, the participants handle a mix of
digital and physical forms, with a trend towards digitization. De-
spite the significant decrease in paper usage and physical mail, the
paperless office is still not quite there yet. They appreciate hav-
ing the documents they need at their fingertips, whether in digital
or paper form. However, searching for documents and retrieving
information is seen as a major time-consuming task.
Collaboration and Communication. These topics are essential
aspects of the participants’ work, which often involves interaction
with different departments. However, this can sometimes lead to
delays, especially when dealing with part-time staff or when waiting
for feedback or decisions (and wasting time chasing responses).
Tools such as MS Teams have simplified many tasks, but they also
note that interruptions for clarification or coordination can disrupt
their work. Clear communication is seen as crucial for efficient task
completion.
Robotics and AI. In general, the participants have a positive view
of robots and AI, seeing potential benefits in their use to automate
mundane tasks. However, they are also concerned about potential

problems such as malfunction, misunderstanding commands, han-
dling sensitive data, and the overreliance on technology that could
lead to a loss of skills. The participants also believe that current
robots will not be able to replace human communication or handle
content-related tasks that require nuanced communication in the
near future.

4.3.2
User Narrative. Based on the findings of the previous anal-
ysis, a user narrative was created to describe the use of a SAR
in an office environment. The user narrative is written from the
user’s perspective, detailing the user’s goals, behaviors, and inter-
action points when utilizing the envisioned robot [31]. It can be
summarized as follows:
The user narrative focuses on a visitor to a fictitious company
that has implemented a receptionless office concept with a SAR
positioned in the entrance area. The visitor, Viktoria Schneider,
CEO of an up-and-coming technology company, experiences initial
confusion and stress upon arrival due to her unfamiliarity with
the building and losing her parking ticket. However, the robot
steps in, verifies Viktoria’s appointment, guides her to the meeting
room, offers her coffee, and solves the problem of the lost parking
ticket. Despite a slight delay, Viktoria’s meeting with the company’s
CEO, Andreas Weber, proceeds successfully, and they negotiate a
cooperation agreement.
The building blocks of the user narrative come directly from the
collected and analysed data. As shown in Fig. 3, key scenes with
central interaction points with the SAR have been identified from
the user narrative. These key scenes not only provide requirements
for the robot itself, but also serve as a guide for further exploration
using prototypes. All subsequent developments can thus be sum-
marized under the umbrella of the user narrative, ensuring later
integration into a coherent overall concept.

4.4
Exploratory Prototyping

This early prototyping phase serves as a means to gain an under-
standing of the wide design space of SARs and to explore what
interaction and design ideas might work within the context of
office environments. As also proposed in [66], we employ these
exploratory techniques to create lightweight, low-cost prototypes,
with the aim of generating early design insights and further refining
the existing results (design-informing models) from the previous
process steps. A depiction of some of these early prototypes can be
seen in Fig. 4.
Cardboard Prototyping. Given that prototyping with actual ro-
botic hardware can consume significant resources, both in terms
of time and cost, we advocate for a more extensive use of low-
fidelity cardboard prototypes. These prototypes offer substantial

DISCUSSION

Based on the survey carried out and a comprehensive analysis of
the state of the art in terms of hardware and software, the fol-
lowing risks can be seen as key challenges when implementing a
collaborative robot system as an assistant in an office context:
Safety. In the course of Industry 4.0 endeavours, safe coopera-
tion between humans (usually production workers) and the robot
system (mobile platforms, grippers, robot arms, extrinsic sensor
systems, etc.) is being enabled and propagated [11]. The previously
closed robotics areas (physical or sensory safety barriers) are in-
creasingly being replaced by spatial collaboration between humans
and machines, which goes hand in hand with much greater safety

requirements. The physical mass of robotic systems and the accel-
eration/speed of movement pose a significant safety risk to humans
or other technical devices. Although fine pressure sensors make it
possible to react immediately to any obstacles, even with a delay
in the millisecond range, the force impact represents a source of
danger for personal injury (impact, incarceration, etc.). Additional
monitoring of the scene (visual or distance/depth sensors) is neces-
sary, but is also not immune to errors, as is increasingly evident in
practical tests of self-driving vehicles [33].
User Acceptance, Effectivity and Efficiency. In the context of
user acceptance, the introduction of assistance systems also har-
bours potential risks. If the SAR does not achieve sufficient accuracy
in the introductory phase or during operation, this will affect both
user acceptance and work effectiveness and efficiency in everyday
office life. If the SAR can be considered significantly error-prone,
the daily work routine will take longer than if the assistance system
were to make work easier. The additional human labour required to
supervise the SAR and manually correct any errors must therefore
be critically and objectively compared with any overall reduction
in workload. This acceptance process has already taken place in
the field of catering assistance systems [20], e.g., Bellabot 2.
Data protection, privacy and surveillance. When introducing
SARs in the office context, aspects of data protection and the pro-
tection of the privacy of both external suppliers/customers and
internal employees must also be taken into account. For appropri-
ate interaction, it is essential that faces are recognised, identified
and recognised again. This is the only way that a pick-up and deliv-
ery service can be implemented appropriately. However, especially
in the area of facial recognition and even more so in the area of
emotional analytics, restrictions are coming into force at the level
of European legislation (cf. EU AI Act) that must also be taken into

Response time and real-time capability. The interaction be-
tween humans and machines not only requires “natural” ways of
communicating, thereby including emotion and affectation, such
as speech, facial expressions and gestures, but first and foremost
places tough real-time demands on the hardware and software.
Particularly in the area of robot-initiated conversation attempts,
it is important to consider that so much time can pass between
recognising the human person and planning the greeting proce-
dure, including the mandatory spatial approach, that the chosen
supposed conversation partner does not even notice the robot’s
approach and therefore does not leave the path it has taken. In this
way of thinking, there would be a worst-case scenario in which
the robot alternately selects targets in a crowded entrance hall
with people hurrying around, but is then too slow to reach them
quickly enough for its approach to be noticed. The result would be a
seemingly haphazard wandering around in the hope of establishing
communication. But even when communication is initiated, a rapid
response time is essential. Speech recognition, speech synthesis and
any facial analysis must take place without any noticeable delay so

that the other person does not lose patience [62] and communica-
tion can take place very naturally.
Seamless integration into the building control system. In addi-
tion to the intuitive and practical interaction with human obstacles,
a mobile robot system in the office environment also faces hard
technical hurdles. Navigating different floors, accessing rooms by
opening doors, overcoming security barriers via an access system
and operating technical devices (e.g., coffee machine) are very high
technical hurdles. Neither a robotic system designed as a mobile
platform with an applied gripper arm, nor a variant designed as a
humanoid can reliably open doors via door buckles or move up and
down stairs in a busy office environment at the current state of the
art. Seamless integration into the building automation system is
therefore essential in order to be able to introduce a robust solution
into live operation. Changing floors will only be possible via API
calls, as will the opening of doors, which must also be supported
by an automated door opening system in terms of hardware and
specific building construction.

6
CONCLUSION

As discussed in this research paper, the development of an inno-
vative robot as an assistance system for everyday office life is a
difficult task in many respects. In the area of technical hardware and
software implementation, in addition to autonomous navigation
and avoiding obstacles, the overcoming of barriers (floor changes,
door barriers, etc.) in particular must be regarded as the most im-
portant sticking points in research and development. In addition
to the difficult hardware solution, the requirements in terms of
human-machine interaction are no less difficult to implement. Only
if communication is intuitive and stable and the robot provides

feedback in a form and manner that is accepted (facial expressions,
tempered speech, confidence-building movements, positive overall
impression, etc.) such a new type of system will find acceptance.
To maximise user-friendliness, effectiveness and efficiency, it is
therefore essential that the cohort of future users, who will commu-
nicate with the robotic system in their daily work, are significantly
integrated into the development steps from the earliest design and
conception phases. This is the only way to create an assistance
system that supports people and does not itself become a “time
waster”, surveillance system or a travelling danger to life and limb.
Based on the developments and findings to date, we are very
confident that in the course of our research project we will be able
to identify the most central pitfalls for office assistance systems in
the context of robotics and, through our user-centred design and
the repeated surveys of the user group, outline a targeted solution
that should also be of great interest for similar developments in the
field of human-machine interaction.
